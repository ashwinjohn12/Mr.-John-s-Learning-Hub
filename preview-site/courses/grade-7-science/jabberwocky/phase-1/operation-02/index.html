SCIENCE YOU WILL USE

Read the environment like a scientist.

Biotic

Living or once-living parts of an environment: organisms, plant material, tracks, waste, remains, and other evidence of life.

Abiotic

Non-living conditions such as temperature, water, light, soil, wind, ice, rock, and other physical factors.

Habitat

The place where an organism lives and gets the resources and conditions it needs to survive.

Evidence

Scientists separate what they directly observe or measure from what they infer from those observations.

EARTH FIELD LINK 01 // HANDS-ON LAB

Microhabitat Environmental Survey

35–50 min

Before trusting remote data from another planet, JCEC requires every Pathfinder team to prove it can collect reliable environmental evidence on Earth.

Equipment

  • Thermometer
  • 1 m string square, metre stick, or four markers
  • Clipboard and pencil
  • Hand lens if available
  • Optional light or soil-moisture sensor

No sensors? Record light as shade / partial / full sun and moisture as dry / damp / wet.

Field safety

  • Stay inside the area your teacher approves.
  • Do not taste, pick, or handle unknown organisms.
  • Observe living things without damaging their habitat.
  • Wash your hands after the investigation.

Procedure

  1. Use three sample points. Use the three sample points your teacher assigns, or choose three different locations if your teacher tells you to.
  2. Mark about 1 m² at each point using string, a metre stick, or visual boundaries.
  3. Measure abiotic evidence: air temperature, light condition, moisture, and one other physical feature you notice.
  4. Observe biotic evidence: count or describe organisms and signs of life without disturbing the site.
  5. Look for human impact: paths, litter, mowing, compacted soil, buildings, noise, shade, drainage, or other changes.
  6. Compare all three samples. Decide which sample appears to provide the greatest variety of conditions or resources for living things and explain why with evidence.

FIELD DEBRIEF

Do not jump from observation to conclusion.

OBSERVE / MEASURE→CLASSIFY→COMPARE→INFER

Example: “The shaded sample was 4°C cooler” is evidence. “More organisms may use the shaded sample because it is cooler and moister” is an inference that must be supported by evidence.
