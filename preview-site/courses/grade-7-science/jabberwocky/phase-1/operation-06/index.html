SCIENCE YOU WILL USE

Recovery is a process—not a reset button.

Disturbance

An event that changes an ecosystem. Disturbance can remove organisms, alter habitat, expose soil, change water flow, or affect resources.

Succession

The gradual change in a biological community after new habitat forms or an existing community is disturbed.

Pioneer organisms

Early colonizers that can establish in disturbed conditions and begin changing the habitat for other organisms.

Secondary succession

Recovery after a disturbance where soil or other parts of the previous ecosystem remain. JCEC's small landing disturbances are examples.

Species richness

The number of different species found in an area. It tells us something different from total plant cover.

Reference site

A nearby less-disturbed area used for comparison. It helps scientists judge how different a recovery zone still is.

Resilience

An ecosystem's ability to respond to disturbance and continue or rebuild important structures, functions, and relationships.

Important: more green cover does not automatically mean the original ecosystem has returned. Scientists also monitor species richness, soil or water conditions, habitat structure, and ecological relationships.
1 Disturbance→2 Soil + some organisms remain→3 Pioneer organisms establish→4 Habitat/resources change→5 More organisms establish→6 Community keeps changing

This is a thinking model, not a fixed recipe. Different ecosystems recover at different rates and may follow different pathways after future disturbances.

HANDS-ON INVESTIGATION // DISTURBANCE & RECOVERY

Surface Cover & Erosion Lab

35–45 min

A disturbance often leaves soil exposed. Your team will compare a bare surface with a protected surface to investigate why recovering vegetation and litter can change what happens during rainfall.

Equipment per team

  • 2 shallow trays or containers
  • equal amounts of soil in both trays
  • dry leaves, grass, shredded paper, mesh, or other teacher-approved cover material
  • 2 collection cups
  • measuring cup with equal water portions
  • books/blocks to create the same gentle slope
  • lab record and pencil

Fair-test variables

  • Change: amount of surface cover
  • Measure: runoff amount and sediment/turbidity
  • Keep the same: soil amount, tray angle, water volume, pour height, pour time, and collection time

Safety: keep water away from Chromebooks, wipe spills immediately, and wash hands after handling soil.

Procedure

  1. Place equal amounts of soil into both trays and set both at the same gentle slope.
  2. Leave Tray A mostly bare. Cover approximately 75% of Tray B with one loose layer of the same teacher-approved material. Do not press the cover deeply into the soil unless your teacher tells you to.
  3. Place a collection cup at the lower end of each tray.
  4. Predict which tray will produce more muddy runoff and explain why.
  5. Pour the same volume of water over Tray A from the same height and over the same amount of time. Repeat for Tray B.
  6. Measure the collected runoff volume. Give each sample a turbidity score using the scale below.
  7. Compare the soil surface after the test. Record channels, displaced soil, pooling, or other changes.
