SCIENCE YOU WILL USE

Introduced does not automatically mean invasive.

Introduced species

An organism moved by humans to a place where it did not previously live.

Invasive species

An introduced species that spreads and causes ecological, economic, or other significant harm. Evidence of both spread and impact matters.

Biosecurity

Actions that prevent organisms, seeds, microbes, or other biological material from being moved where they could cause harm.

Pollutant

A substance released into the environment that can harm organisms or change ecological conditions.

Bioaccumulation

When a persistent substance builds up inside an organism over time because it enters faster than it leaves.

Biomagnification

When the concentration of a persistent substance becomes greater at higher feeding levels in a food chain or web.

JCEC evidence rule: do not call an introduced organism invasive just because it came from Earth. Look for evidence that it is spreading and that it is changing native organisms, habitat, or ecosystem processes.
About X-17: X-17 is a fictional JCEC maintenance tracer used in this mission. The numbers model how a persistent pollutant can move through a real food web; they are not measurements of a real chemical.

HANDS-ON INVESTIGATION A // BIOSECURITY

Stowaway Spread Simulation

25–35 min

A seed, egg, spore, or tiny organism does not need to move on purpose. Cargo, soil, boots, wheels, tools, and water can move it. Your team will compare spread without inspection and spread with a biosecurity checkpoint.

Equipment per team

  • 6 cups or paper habitat zones labelled Cargo, A, B, C, D, E
  • about 30 neutral counters
  • 4 clearly different “stowaway” counters
  • 1 spoon or small scoop
  • Incident Record

Model rule

Neutral counters represent ordinary cargo/material. Different-coloured counters represent hidden biological stowaways. No counter represents a real organism in the classroom.

Random transfer matters: do not deliberately choose or avoid stowaway counters unless the procedure says to inspect them.

Round A — No biosecurity checkpoint

  1. Place all 30 neutral counters and 4 stowaway counters in Cargo. Mix without looking.
  2. Move exactly 5 random counters from Cargo → A. Record whether A now contains a stowaway.
  3. Mix A and move 3 random counters A → B. Then B → C, C → D, and D → E using the same rule.
  4. Count how many habitat zones contain at least one stowaway.

Round B — Add a biosecurity checkpoint

  1. Reset the counters exactly as they began.
  2. Repeat the same transfers, but inspect each transfer before it enters the next zone. Remove any visible stowaway counter to a quarantine cup.
  3. Count how many zones contain a stowaway now.
  4. Compare the two rounds. Which strategy prevented spread earlier?

Team conclusion

Why can prevention be easier than removing an organism after it spreads?

Model limitation: real organisms reproduce, die, disperse in different ways, and interact with habitat. This simulation isolates one idea: human movement can create pathways for spread, and checkpoints can interrupt those pathways.

HANDS-ON INVESTIGATION B // CONTAMINANT TRANSFER

Biomagnification Token Model

20–30 min

This model assumes the contaminant is persistent: organisms take it in and do not quickly remove it. The tokens show contaminant amount moving with food.

1. Producers

Create 16 producer cards. Give each producer 1 contaminant token.

2. Primary consumers

Create 4 consumer cards. Each consumer “eats” 4 producers and collects all 4 contaminant tokens.

3. Predator

One predator “eats” all 4 consumers and collects all 16 contaminant tokens.

4. Compare

Producer: 1 token each → Consumer: 4 each → Predator: 16. Explain why the concentration per organism increased.

Important: biomagnification does not happen for every substance. It is most relevant when a contaminant persists in organisms and is transferred through feeding.
