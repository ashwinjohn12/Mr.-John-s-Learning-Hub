SCIENCE YOU WILL USE

A count is evidence—not automatically the whole population.

Population

All members of one species living in a particular area at a particular time.

Population distribution

Where members of a population are located. A population can move or become clustered without necessarily changing total size.

Limiting factor

A condition or resource that can restrict population growth or survival, such as food, water, shelter, space, temperature, or another interaction.

Trend

The overall pattern in data over time—such as increasing, decreasing, stable, or changing direction.

Sample

A smaller part of a population or habitat used to estimate or describe the larger system. How and where we sample matters.

Correlation ≠ proof of cause

Two patterns changing together can support an explanation, but scientists also look for timing, mechanism, other evidence, and alternative explanations.

JCEC rule: never write “the graph proves…” when the evidence only supports a likely explanation. Use precise language such as supports, suggests, is consistent with, may, likely, and because.

HANDS-ON INVESTIGATION // ECOLOGICAL MONITORING

Population Sampling Lab

35–45 min

Ecologists often cannot count every organism. Your team will use random quadrat samples to estimate an unknown population, then test what happens when the same population becomes clustered.

Equipment per team

  • 1 printed 5 × 5 habitat grid
  • 1 cup of small counters, beans, beads, or paper dots prepared by your teacher
  • Calculator if permitted
  • Population Sampling Record

Do not count the whole cup first. The unknown total is part of the investigation.

Team jobs

  • Sampler: counts only the selected quadrats.
  • Recorder: records counts and calculations.
  • Randomizer: generates sample-square numbers.
  • Quality Checker: checks that the team does not choose “good-looking” squares on purpose.

Your teacher may assign or combine jobs. Everyone should be able to explain why random sampling matters.

RANDOM QUADRAT SELECTOR

Keep both rounds visible.

Generate Round 1 only after the counters are spread. Record it on paper before changing the distribution.

ROUND 1 SAMPLE— — — — —
ROUND 2 SAMPLE— — — — —

Generate Round 1 first. Round 2 unlocks after Round 1 has been generated.

Round 1 — Estimate the unknown population

  1. Pour the counters onto the habitat grid without counting them. Make sure each counter lies inside one numbered square. If a counter lands on a grid line, gently move it into the square containing most of the counter.
  2. Generate five random quadrat numbers. Record the Round 1 sample on your sheet. Do not replace numbers with squares that look more interesting.
  3. Count only those five squares and record each count.
  4. Calculate the mean number per quadrat: total organisms in the five samples ÷ 5.
  5. Estimate the whole population: mean per quadrat × 25 quadrats.
  6. Now count every counter to reveal the true population. Calculate how far your estimate was from the true count.

Round 2 — Same population, different distribution

  1. Do not add or remove any counters. Move many of them into a cluster of several neighbouring quadrats while leaving other areas sparse.
  2. Only after the counters are clustered, generate the Round 2 sample. Your Round 1 numbers remain visible above for comparison.
  3. Repeat the estimate and compare Round 1 with Round 2. The true population stayed the same. Did the estimate change? Why?
Sampling error

An estimate can be above or below the true population because a sample is only part of the habitat.

Distribution matters

Clustered organisms can make a small number of quadrats less representative of the whole area.

Repeated evidence helps

More samples, repeated surveys, and consistent methods usually give scientists more confidence.

MISSION CHECKPOINT // BEFORE OPENING THE CASE

Three questions before you trust a population graph.

1

What was actually measured? A survey count, detection index, or sample is not automatically the total population.

2

Was the method consistent? Changes in time, place, weather, or equipment can change what gets detected.

3

What other evidence changes at the same time? Look for resources, abiotic conditions, movement, predators, and alternative explanations.

JCEC POPULATION ALERT // DATA REVIEW REQUIRED

Open your assigned population case.

Do not choose a cause after looking at one graph. Read all datasets, check the monitoring notes, compare the timing, and then decide which explanation is most defensible.

DETECTIONS ≠ TOTAL POPULATIONA detection count can change for more than one reason.
  • the population size changed;
  • organisms moved to a different place;
  • their behaviour or activity changed;
  • habitat conditions changed where they were found;
  • or the monitoring method detected them differently.

Your job: decide which explanation—or combination of explanations—the evidence supports.

STEP 4 // BUILD YOUR CER

What does the two-site evidence suggest about where Reedcrawlers moved as the wetland changed?

QUICK CER EXAMPLE // NOT FROM JABBERWOCKY

Claim

The shaded microhabitat likely supported more of the organisms observed during our survey.

Evidence

Sample A had 12 organisms while Sample B had 5, and Sample A was 4°C cooler.

Reasoning

The cooler conditions may reduce water loss and provide conditions that more of the observed organisms could tolerate.

Notice: Evidence tells what was observed or measured. Reasoning explains why that evidence supports the claim.
C
Claim

State the explanation your team thinks best fits the population pattern. Do not claim more than the data support.

E
Evidence

Use at least three specific pieces of evidence. Include exact values or changes from at least two datasets.

R
Reasoning

Explain why the evidence supports the claim using population, limiting-factor, habitat, resource, or interaction science.

A strong team explanation also includes:
  • one alternative explanation you considered;
  • why current evidence supports it less strongly, or what evidence would be needed to test it;
  • one uncertainty or next measurement.
