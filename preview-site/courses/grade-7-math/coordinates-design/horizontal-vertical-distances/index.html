Horizontal and Vertical Distances

Alberta Grade 7 · Shape & Space SS5: Lesson 1.4 develops the remaining movement-and-distance indicators: describe the horizontal and vertical movement required to move from one point to another; describe the positional change from vertices to corresponding image vertices after a transformation or successive transformations; and determine distance between points along horizontal and vertical lines. Performing transformations and identifying image coordinates were developed in Lesson 1.3 and are used here only as prior knowledge.

Quick start:Point A moves from (−4, 3) to (2, 3). The movement is 6 units right. The horizontal distance is 6 units. Why does only one statement include a direction?
Check

Movement includes direction. Distance describes how far and is non-negative.

🌎

START HERE

Movement and distance answer different questions

Compare x with x for horizontal change and y with y for vertical change.

Movement = amount + direction

6 units left tells both how far and which way the point changed position.

Distance = how far

6 units is a non-negative magnitude. Distance itself is not “negative 6.”

Correspondence matters

Track A → A′ → A″. Compare each original vertex with its own image—not whichever point happens to be closest.

Same finish, different route

Two routes can have the same net movement but different total horizontal or vertical distance travelled because one route may backtrack.

📘

LEARN

A reliable horizontal–vertical routine

1. Start and finish: read coordinates carefully

Write the starting point and finishing point. Keep the order (x, y). The x-values tell the horizontal story; the y-values tell the vertical story.

Routine: x → horizontal, y → vertical.

2. Describe horizontal movement

Compare the starting x-coordinate with the finishing x-coordinate. State the number of units and a direction: right if the final x-value is greater, left if it is smaller, and no horizontal movement if they match.

Common error: using the sign of the final coordinate as the direction. Crossing zero does not change the routine.

3. Describe vertical movement

Compare the starting y-coordinate with the finishing y-coordinate. State the number of units and up or down. If the y-values match, the vertical movement is 0 units.

4. Determine horizontal or vertical distance

If two points lie on the same horizontal line, compare their x-coordinates to find the horizontal distance. If two points lie on the same vertical line, compare their y-coordinates to find the vertical distance. Report a non-negative number of units.

Movement: 6 units left. Distance: 6 units.

5. Track corresponding vertices through successive transformations

After one transformation, A becomes A′. After a second transformation, A′ becomes A″. To describe the overall positional change, compare the original A with the final A″. Repeat for B → B′ → B″ and every other corresponding vertex.

6. Separate net movement from distance travelled

Net movement compares only the starting and finishing positions. Total distance travelled adds the non-negative horizontal or vertical amount from every step. If a route backtracks, total distance travelled can be larger than the net movement.

Scope boundary: this lesson compares horizontal and vertical change only. No other distance method is introduced.

🧩

WATCH THE THINKING

Worked examples

Example 1 · Same horizontal line

P(−6, 3) and Q(4, 3) share y = 3.

COORDINATE MODEL

Horizontal distance example

x first: move left or right. y second: move down or up.

Movement P → Q: 10 units right. Horizontal distance: 10 units.

Example 2 · Cross zero in both directions

A(5, −4) becomes A′(−2, 3).

COORDINATE MODEL

Cross-zero positional change

x first: move left or right. y second: move down or up.

x: 5 → −2 gives 7 left. y: −4 → 3 gives 7 up. Horizontal and vertical distances are each 7 units.

Example 3 · Successive images

A → A′ → A″: (−4, 2) → (3, 2) → (1, −5).

Step 1: 7 right, 0 vertical. Step 2: 2 left, 7 down. Overall A → A″: 5 right and 7 down. Horizontal distance travelled along the route is 9 units, while the net horizontal movement is only 5 units right.

Example 4 · Corresponding vertices can change differently

After a rotation followed by a translation, A → A″ may move 4 left and 6 up while B → B″ moves 4 left and 12 up. A transformation sequence acts on the whole shape, but different original vertices can have different overall positional changes.

Example 5 · Same finish, two routes

Route A and Route B both start at (−5, 4) and finish at (4, −3). Both therefore have the same net change: 9 right, 7 down. If Route B detours farther before returning, its total distance travelled can be larger.
