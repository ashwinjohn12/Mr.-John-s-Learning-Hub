LESSON 5.1 · GRADE 6 MATH

Fractions, Decimals & Percents

Different ways to describe the same amount—and use percent to find a part, a percent, or a whole.

START HERE

The big idea

A percentage is a part-whole relationship written as an equivalent ratio out of 100.

Fractions, decimal numbers, ratios, and percentages can describe the same amount in different ways. For example, 1/4, 0.25, 25 : 100, and 25% all describe the same part of a whole. Percentage problems are proportional: when one side of the relationship is scaled, the other side must be scaled by the same factor. You can use this idea to find a part, a percent, or the whole without relying on a shortcut you do not understand.

LEARN

Build your understanding

01

Percent means “out of 100”

The symbol % means percent, or “per hundred.” A percentage compares a part with a whole of 100. So 37% means 37 out of 100, which can be written as 37/100 or 0.37.

Remember: The percent tells a relationship, not a fixed amount. 25% can represent different amounts depending on the whole.

02

Fractions, decimals, and percentages can name the same amount

The same part-whole relationship can be represented in several forms. For example, 3/4 = 75/100 = 0.75 = 75%. Moving between forms helps you choose a useful strategy for a problem.

Remember: Changing the representation does not change the amount.

03

A proportion keeps the relationship equal

A proportion shows two equivalent ratios. If 20% of a group is being described, you can think of 20 : 100 and compare it with part : whole. Scale both parts of the relationship by the same factor so the ratios stay equivalent.

25 : 100=15 : 60
25 → 15 × 0.6100 → 60 × 0.6
Both numbers scale the same way, so the ratio stays equivalent.

Remember: Do the same scaling to both parts of the ratio. Do not change only one number.

04

Benchmark percentages make mental math easier

Useful benchmarks include 50% = one-half, 25% = one-quarter, 10% = one-tenth, 5% = half of 10%, and 1% = one-hundredth. More complicated percentages can often be built from these. For example, 15% = 10% + 5%.

Remember: Look for a friendly percentage before reaching for a longer calculation.

05

You might be finding the part, the percent, or the whole

Read the question before calculating. “What is 30% of 80?” asks for the part. “18 is what percent of 60?” asks for the percent. “12 is 25% of what number?” asks for the whole. The relationship is the same, but the missing value changes.

Remember: Name the missing piece first: part, percent, or whole.

Using percent in problems

Percent of a number can be calculated in more than one correct way

You can use a proportion, a benchmark strategy, or multiply the whole by the percentage and divide by 100. For 30% of 60: 60 × 30 ÷ 100 = 18. A benchmark method gives the same result because 10% of 60 is 6, so 30% is 3 × 6 = 18.

Remember: A good strategy should make the proportional relationship visible and give a reasonable answer.

Real-life percent problems often have more than one step

A discount tells the amount taken off, not automatically the final price. A tax tells the extra amount added. Read the wording carefully, calculate the percentage amount, and then decide whether to add or subtract it from the original amount.

Remember: Discount: subtract. Tax: add.

COMMON MIX-UPS

“25% of a number means subtract 25.”

Twenty-five percent is a relationship, not the number 25. For a whole of 60, 25% is one-quarter of 60, which is 15.

“If the percent is 40%, the answer must be 40.”

The percent describes how large the part is compared with the whole. Forty percent of 25 is 10, while 40% of 200 is 80.

“A 20% discount means the final price is 20% of the original.”

A 20% discount removes 20% of the original price, so 80% of the original price remains.

“I can scale one side of a proportion but leave the other side alone.”

That changes the relationship. Equivalent ratios stay equivalent only when both terms are scaled by the same factor.

“I should always cross-multiply.”

Cross-multiplication can hide the relationship you are trying to understand. In Grade 6, focus on equivalent ratios, benchmark percentages, and scaling both parts of the proportion.

WATCH THE THINKING

Worked examples

Start with the two core examples. Then open Examples 3 and 4 to see how to find a missing percent and a missing whole.

Example 1 · Same amount, four forms

Write 3/4 as a ratio out of 100, a decimal number, and a percentage.

  1. Make an equivalent fraction with denominator 100: 3/4 = 75/100.
  2. Read 75/100 as the ratio 75 : 100.
  3. Write 75/100 as a decimal: 0.75.
  4. Because percent means out of 100, 75/100 = 75%.
Answer: 3/4 = 75/100 = 0.75 = 75%

Example 2 · Find the part

Find 30% of 60.

  1. Identify the whole: 60. The missing value is the part.
  2. Use a benchmark: 10% of 60 is 6.
  3. Thirty percent is three groups of 10%, so 3 × 6 = 18.
  4. Check: 18 is less than half of 60, which makes sense because 30% is less than 50%.
Answer: 18
Ready for more? Open Examples 3 and 4

Example 3 · Find the percent

18 is what percent of 60?

  1. Identify the part and whole: the part is 18 and the whole is 60.
  2. Write the relationship as 18/60 and simplify it to 3/10.
  3. Write the fraction out of 100: 3/10 = 30/100.
  4. Because percent means out of 100, 30/100 = 30%.
  5. Check: 18 is a little less than one-third of 60, so 30% is reasonable.
Answer: 30%

Example 3 · Find the whole

12 is 25% of what number?

  1. The missing value is the whole.
  2. Recognize that 25% = 1/4.
  3. If one-quarter of the whole is 12, four equal quarters make the whole.
  4. Calculate 12 × 4 = 48.
Answer: 48

OFF-SCREEN ACTIVITY

Build a 100-Square Percent Model

  1. Draw or print a 10 × 10 grid so there are 100 equal squares.
  2. Shade 25 squares. Label the model as 25/100, 0.25, and 25%.
  3. Shade a new grid to show 40%. Write a simplified fraction and decimal for the same amount.
  4. Choose a percentage that can be built from 10% and 5%, such as 15%, 35%, or 65%. Explain how your shading connects to the benchmark percentages.
  5. Create one challenge for someone else: give either the part, the percent, or the whole and leave one value missing.

Reflect: How does the 100-square model help explain why the percent stays the same even when the real-life whole is not 100?

Working on your own? Draw the 10 × 10 grid on paper. It does not need to be perfect—just make sure it has 100 equal spaces.

LESSON SUMMARY

  • Percent means a comparison to 100.
  • Fractions, decimals, ratios, and percentages can describe the same part-whole relationship.
  • Equivalent ratios and benchmark percentages help you find a percent of a number.
  • A percentage problem may ask for the part, the percent, or the whole.
  • Discounts are subtracted from the original amount; taxes are added.
  • Reasonable estimates help catch mistakes.

PAT-STYLE THINKING

A student says that 25% of 80 is 25 because “25% means 25 out of 100.” Which response best explains the error? A) 25% always equals 20. B) The 25 describes a ratio out of 100; the actual part depends on the whole, so 25% of 80 is 20. C) Percent cannot be used when the whole is not 100. D) 25% of 80 is found by 80 − 25.

Show answer

B. The percent describes the relationship. Since 25% is one-quarter, one-quarter of 80 is 20.
