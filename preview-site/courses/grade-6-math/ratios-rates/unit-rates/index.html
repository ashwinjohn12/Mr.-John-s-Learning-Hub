START HERE

The big idea

A unit rate tells how much of one quantity there is for 1 unit of another quantity.

Rates compare quantities with different units, such as dollars and notebooks or kilometres and hours. A unit rate makes the second quantity 1, which creates a common basis for comparison. To find it, scale both parts of the rate so the second term becomes 1. Then use that per-one relationship to compare or extend the situation.

1Name the unitsWhat two quantities are being compared?
2Make it per 1Scale the second term to 1.
3Use the rateCompare or extend the relationship.
Think about itWhy is “$4 for 5 apples” harder to compare with “$5.25 for 7 apples” than their prices per apple?

LEARN

Build your understanding

01

A rate compares different units

Rates compare quantities measured in different units, such as dollars and items, kilometres and hours, or pages and days.

Remember: Say the units when you read the rate.

02

A unit rate has a second term of 1

A rate such as $12 for 3 books can be rewritten as $4 for 1 book. The unit rate is $4 per book.

Remember: Unit rate means “per 1.”

03

Divide both quantities to make the second term 1

For $15 : 5 notebooks, divide both terms by 5. The equivalent rate is $3 : 1 notebook.

Remember: This is still equivalent-ratio thinking.

04

The order and units tell you what the rate means

A rate should match the question you are trying to answer. For example, $3 per notebook tells the cost of 1 notebook, while 3 notebooks per dollar answers a different question. Keep the units visible and choose the order that communicates the situation clearly.

Remember: Ask: “What do I want to know per 1?”

Want to go deeper? Reverse the rate

5 km/h tells distance per hour. The related inverse rate 0.2 h/km tells time per kilometre. Both can describe the same trip, but they answer different questions. In this lesson, use the form that best matches what the question asks.

05

Unit rates make comparisons fair

Different package sizes can be compared using cost per item. Different trips can be compared using distance per hour.

Remember: Compare like with like.

Using a unit rate to extend a relationship

A unit rate can extend the relationship

If a car travels 90 km per hour, then in 3 hours it travels 270 km. First find “per 1,” then scale to the new quantity.

Remember: Per 1 can be a bridge to any amount.

VISUAL MODEL · PER 1

Unit rate is an equivalent rate

$20 : 5 notebooks÷5$4 : 1 notebook

Both quantities were divided by 5, so the relationship stayed equivalent while the second term became 1.

COMMON MIX-UPS

“The cheaper total price is always the better deal.”

Package sizes may differ. Compare the cost for the same quantity, often by finding the price per item.

“I can ignore the units.”

Units tell what each number means. $3/notebook is different from 3 notebooks/$1.

“The larger unit rate is always better.”

It depends on what is being compared. Lower cost per item is usually better, while greater distance per hour means faster speed.

“Unit rate is a new trick.”

It is the same equivalent-ratio reasoning from Lesson 5.2, with one term scaled to 1.

WATCH THE THINKING

Worked examples

Start with the two core examples. Open the next examples when you are ready to apply the same reasoning in a new situation.

Example 1 · Find a unit price

Five notebooks cost $20. What is the unit price?

  1. Write the rate: $20 : 5 notebooks.
  2. Make the notebook term 1 by dividing by 5.
  3. Divide the cost by the same number: $20 ÷ 5 = $4.
  4. Read the result using “per.”
Answer: $4 per notebook

Example 2 · Compare speeds

Janice runs 10 km in 2 h. Mary runs 18 km in 3 h. Who is faster?

  1. Janice: 10 ÷ 2 = 5 km per hour.
  2. Mary: 18 ÷ 3 = 6 km per hour.
  3. Compare the same unit rates: 6 km/h > 5 km/h.
Answer: Mary is faster.
Ready for more? Open Examples 3 and 4

Example 3 · Use the unit rate to extend

A car travels 180 km in 2 h. How far will it travel in 3 h at the same rate?

  1. Find the unit rate: 180 ÷ 2 = 90 km/h.
  2. Use the per-one rate for 3 hours: 90 × 3 = 270.
  3. Check that 180 : 2 and 270 : 3 describe the same rate.
Answer: 270 km

Example 4 · Best buy

Deal A is 2 chocolate bars for $2.50. Deal B is 3 bars for $3.00. Which has the lower unit price?

  1. Deal A: $2.50 ÷ 2 = $1.25 per bar.
  2. Deal B: $3.00 ÷ 3 = $1.00 per bar.
  3. For price, the lower cost per item is the better deal.
Answer: Deal B at $1.00 per bar

LESSON SUMMARY

  • A rate compares quantities with different units.
  • A unit rate has a second term of 1.
  • Unit rates are equivalent rates made by scaling to “per 1.”
  • Unit price and speed are common unit rates.
  • Unit rates help compare options and extend proportional relationships.
Need more help?
  • Write the units beside every number.
  • Ask what you want to know “per 1.”
  • Use a proportion or ratio table if dividing directly feels unclear.
  • Check by multiplying the unit rate back to the original quantity.
Want an extra challenge?
  • Find both dollars per item and items per dollar for the same deal and compare what each tells you.
  • Create three package deals where the middle total price is the best unit price.
  • Compare two speeds given in different time intervals.
  • Explain when a larger unit rate is better and when a smaller unit rate is better.

PAT-STYLE THINKING

Two stores sell the same notebook. Store A sells 3 for $6.00. Store B sells 5 for $9.00. A student chooses Store A because $6 is less than $9. Explain why that comparison is incomplete and identify the better deal.

Show a strong answer

The package sizes are different, so total price alone is not a fair comparison. Store A is $2.00 per notebook. Store B is $1.80 per notebook. Store B has the lower unit price and is the better deal if price per notebook is the only factor.
