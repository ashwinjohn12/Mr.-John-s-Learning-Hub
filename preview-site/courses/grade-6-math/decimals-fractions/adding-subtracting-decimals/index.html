Adding & Subtracting Decimal Numbers

START HERE

The big idea

Decimal addition and subtraction work because we combine or compare the same place-value units.

This connects directly to fractions. In Lesson 4.1, fractions had to use the same-sized fractional units before they could be combined. Decimal numbers already use named base-ten units such as ones, tenths, hundredths, and thousandths. When we line up decimal points, we are really lining up equal place-value units. The standard algorithms are efficient because each column combines or compares the same kind of unit.

LEARN

Build your understanding

01

Fractions and decimals both name units

A decimal such as 2.37 can be read as 2 ones, 3 tenths, and 7 hundredths. Just as thirds and fourths are different fractional units, tenths and hundredths are different place-value units. Strong decimal work starts by noticing what each digit represents.

Remember: The decimal point separates the ones place from the fractional place-value units.

02

Align by place value, not by the last digit

For 12.45 + 8.7632, place the ones under the ones, tenths under tenths, and so on. Lining up the decimal points is a fast way to guarantee that the place values match.

Remember: Do not slide one number so the final digits line up. Match the decimal points and place values instead.

03

Placeholder zeros can make the units visible

12.45, 12.450, and 12.4500 are equal values. Adding zeros to the right of a decimal number does not change its value, but it can make subtraction easier to organize: 12.4500 − 8.7632.

Remember: A zero to the right of the final decimal digit can be useful without changing the number.

04

Regrouping is still a place-value exchange

If there are not enough hundredths to subtract, regroup 1 tenth as 10 hundredths. If needed, regroup 1 one as 10 tenths. The decimal point does not stop regrouping; it simply marks where the place-value units change.

Remember: Regrouping across the decimal point follows the same base-ten idea as regrouping with natural numbers.

05

Whole-number and decimal calculations can be scaled versions of each other

153 + 481 = 634. Because 15.3 and 48.1 are each one tenth as large, 15.3 + 48.1 = 63.4, also one tenth as large. Place-value scaling helps you predict where the decimal belongs and whether an answer is reasonable.

Remember: If every number in a sum is divided by 10, the sum is also divided by 10.

06

The story tells you which operation to use

A total amount usually requires combining quantities, while a comparison or change may require subtraction. But do not depend only on words such as “more,” “left,” or “difference.” Ask what quantities are known and what the problem is asking you to find.

Remember: Represent the situation before calculating.

07

Estimation is an error detector

Before calculating 47.83 + 12.12, you can estimate 48 + 12 ≈ 60. An exact answer near 60 is reasonable; an answer such as 599.5 or 6.0 would signal a place-value mistake.

Remember: A quick estimate can catch a misplaced decimal point even when your algorithm looks neat.

COMMON MIX-UPS

“Line up the last digits.”

In 5.8 + 0.47, the 8 is in the tenths place and the 7 is in the hundredths place. They should not be placed in the same column. Write 5.80 + 0.47 so equal place values line up.

“Adding a zero changes the value.”

5.8 and 5.80 name the same amount. The extra zero says there are 0 additional hundredths; it does not add a new quantity.

“The decimal point can be placed after I finish.”

Decimal placement is not a decoration added at the end. The decimal point organizes the place-value columns before and during the calculation.

“More decimal digits means a larger number.”

3.8 is greater than 3.745 even though 3.745 has more digits. Compare equal place values: 3.800 > 3.745.

“For subtraction, subtract the smaller digit from the larger digit in each column.”

The order of the numbers matters. Use regrouping when the digit in the top number is too small; do not reverse the subtraction inside one column.

WATCH THE THINKING

Worked examples

Start with the place-value connection and a direct decimal sum. Open the next examples when you are ready for subtraction, money, race times, and a larger data comparison.

Example 1 · Scale a familiar calculation

How are 153 + 481 and 15.3 + 48.1 related?

  1. Calculate the natural-number sum: 153 + 481 = 634.
  2. 15.3 is one tenth of 153 and 48.1 is one tenth of 481.
  3. So the decimal sum should be one tenth of 634.
  4. 15.3 + 48.1 = 63.4.
Answer: 63.4; the decimal sum is one tenth of 634.

Example 2 · Add different decimal lengths

Find 12.45 + 8.7632.

  1. Line up the decimal points so ones, tenths, hundredths, and thousandths match.
  2. You may write 12.45 as 12.4500 to make the columns easier to see.
  3. Add from the smallest place value toward the left, regrouping when needed.
  4. The sum is 21.2132.
Answer: 21.2132
Ready for the next level? Open Examples 3–6

Example 3 · Subtract with placeholder zeros

Find 12.45 − 8.7632.

  1. Rewrite 12.45 as 12.4500 so both numbers show the same place-value columns.
  2. Align the decimal points.
  3. Regroup through the place-value columns as needed.
  4. Subtract to get 3.6868.
Answer: 3.6868

Example 4 · Money and change

A student buys chips for $2.50, two chocolate bars at $1.75 each, and a drink for $2.25. How much change from $20?

  1. Find the cost of two chocolate bars: $1.75 + $1.75 = $3.50.
  2. Add all purchases: $2.50 + $3.50 + $2.25 = $8.25.
  3. Subtract the total cost from $20.00.
  4. $20.00 − $8.25 = $11.75.
Answer: $11.75 change

Example 5 · Race-time improvement

Anya swims 28.457 s at one meet and 27.989 s at the next. By how much did her time improve?

  1. A lower race time is faster, so improvement means the time decreased.
  2. Subtract the new time from the old time: 28.457 − 27.989.
  3. Align the decimal points and regroup.
  4. The difference is 0.468 seconds.
Answer: 0.468 s faster

Example 6 · Compare population changes

Grande Prairie changes from 64,141 to 69,377 people. Red Deer changes from 100,844 to 112,917. Which increase is greater?

  1. Grande Prairie increase: 69,377 − 64,141 = 5,236.
  2. Red Deer increase: 112,917 − 100,844 = 12,073.
  3. Compare the two increases.
  4. 12,073 is greater than 5,236.
Answer: Red Deer, with an increase of 12,073 people.

REAL-LIFE CHALLENGE

You are helping organize a school field day. The snack station starts with a $75.00 supply budget. It spends $18.75 on fruit, $12.60 on drinks, and $9.95 on cups. A swimmer records 28.457 s in one trial and 27.989 s in the next.

  1. Estimate the total snack-station spending before calculating exactly.
  2. Find the exact total spent and show how you aligned the decimal numbers.
  3. Find how much of the $75.00 budget remains.
  4. Determine how much the swimmer’s time improved.
  5. Explain why each answer is reasonable using place value or estimation.
Check the core ideas

Estimate: about $19 + $13 + $10 = $42. Exact spending: $18.75 + $12.60 + $9.95 = $41.30. Budget remaining: $75.00 − $41.30 = $33.70. Time improvement: 28.457 − 27.989 = 0.468 s. Each exact answer is close to a sensible estimate.

WRAP UP

Ready to move on?

LESSON SUMMARY

  • Decimal addition and subtraction are organized by place value.
  • Aligning decimal points lines up equal place-value units.
  • Trailing placeholder zeros can make place values visible without changing value.
  • Regrouping across the decimal point is still a base-ten exchange.
  • The meaning of the situation determines whether to add or subtract.
  • Estimation helps detect misplaced decimals and other unreasonable answers.

IF YOU NEED SUPPORT

  • Use a place-value chart and write one digit in each column.
  • Rewrite shorter decimals with placeholder zeros before calculating.
  • Underline the decimal points before you align the numbers.
  • Estimate with whole numbers first, then compare your exact answer with the estimate.

IF YOU WANT A CHALLENGE

  • Create two different decimal addition problems with an answer between 9.99 and 10.01.
  • Find two decimal numbers whose difference is exactly 0.001 and explain their place-value relationship.
  • Explain why multiplying both 15.3 and 48.1 by 10 also multiplies their sum by 10.
  • Write a real-life problem where a smaller decimal number represents a better result, such as a race time.

PAT-STYLE THINKING

A student calculates 5.8 + 0.47 and gets 5.127. Explain the mistake and give the correct sum.

Show answer

The student lined up the final digits instead of equal place values. Write 5.8 as 5.80, then align the decimal points: 5.80 + 0.47 = 6.27. The 8 tenths must be combined with the 4 tenths, not the 7 hundredths.
