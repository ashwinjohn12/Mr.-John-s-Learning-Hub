LESSON 4.2 · GRADE 6 MATH

Multiplying Fractions

Interpret and calculate multiplication between natural numbers and fractions using models, repeated addition, and part-of reasoning.

START HERE

The big idea

Multiplying a natural number and a fraction can mean repeated groups of a fraction or taking a fractional part of a quantity.

In Grade 6, the focus is multiplication between natural numbers and fractions—not a general fraction × fraction algorithm. A natural number times a fraction can be understood as repeated addition. A fraction times a natural number can be understood as taking part of a quantity. These expressions have the same product because multiplication is commutative, but the models tell different stories. Multiplication does not always make a number larger: a proper fractional factor makes a positive natural-number quantity smaller, while an improper fractional factor can make it larger.

LEARN

Build your understanding

01

Repeated addition builds fraction multiplication

The expression 4 × 2/5 means four groups of 2/5. Repeated addition shows the meaning: 2/5 + 2/5 + 2/5 + 2/5 = 8/5. The denominator stays 5 because the size of each fractional unit has not changed.

Remember: The natural number tells how many equal groups of the fraction you have.

02

A natural number can multiply a unit fraction

For 6 × 1/4, think of six groups of one fourth. The product is 6/4, which simplifies to 3/2 or 1 1/2. You are counting fourths, so the denominator remains 4.

Remember: Multiply the numerator by the natural number; do not multiply the denominator by the natural number.

03

A fraction of a quantity is another multiplication meaning

The expression 3/5 × 20 asks for three-fifths of 20. Split 20 into 5 equal parts first: 20 ÷ 5 = 4. Then take 3 of those parts: 3 × 4 = 12.

Remember: The denominator tells how many equal parts to make; the numerator tells how many of those parts to keep.

04

Groups-of and part-of are different models

4 × 2/5 can be shown as four groups of 2/5 on a number line. 2/5 × 4 can be shown by partitioning a quantity of 4 into fifths and keeping two-fifths. The product is equal because multiplication is commutative, but a strong explanation can tell which model fits the situation.

Remember: Same answer does not mean the two stories or diagrams look the same.

05

Multiplication does not always make a number larger

If the fractional factor is between 0 and 1, you are taking only part of a positive quantity, so the product is smaller than the natural number. If the fractional factor is exactly 1, the size stays the same. If the fractional factor is greater than 1, the product can be greater than the natural number.

Remember: Predict the product size before calculating. It is a powerful error check.

06

Multiplying by a unit fraction is division

Taking one-fourth of 12 means dividing 12 into 4 equal parts. So 1/4 × 12 = 12 ÷ 4 = 3. This is why multiplication by a unit fraction connects naturally to division.

Remember: a × 1/n and a ÷ n describe the same equal-sharing amount.

07

Fraction multiplication can appear inside larger expressions

A problem may require you to multiply first and then add or subtract. Parentheses can show which amount is being changed. Use the same order-of-operations reasoning you learned in Unit 3.

Remember: Write one expression that matches the story before calculating.

VISUAL MODEL · GROUPS OF A FRACTION

The number of pieces changes; the piece size does not

Three groups of 2/5 can be read as repeated addition. Every piece is still a fifth.

2/5 + 2/5 + 2/53 × 2/56/5 = 1 1/5

Why this matters: The denominator stays 5 because the units are still fifths. Only the count of fifths changes.

COMMON MIX-UPS

“Multiply the numerator and denominator by the natural number.”

For 3 × 2/5, multiplying both parts would give 6/15, which is only 2/5 again. Repeated addition shows that three groups of 2/5 make 6/5, not 6/15.

“Multiplication always makes numbers bigger.”

Multiplying 12 by 1/3 gives 4 because one-third means only one of three equal parts of 12. A factor between 0 and 1 can make a positive quantity smaller.

“4 × 2/5 and 2/5 × 4 must use the same model.”

They have the same product, but the first naturally shows four groups of 2/5 while the second naturally shows two-fifths of a quantity of 4.

“The denominator changes when I add more groups.”

If you repeatedly add fifths, the pieces are still fifths. Only the number of fifths changes.

“This lesson means fraction × fraction.”

Not yet. In Grade 6, this lesson focuses on multiplication between fractions and natural numbers. General fraction × fraction multiplication comes later.

WATCH THE THINKING

Worked examples

Start with repeated groups and fraction-of-a-quantity. Open the next examples when you are ready for prediction, division, and multi-step reasoning.

Example 1 · Repeated groups

Find 4 × 2/5 and explain what it means.

  1. Read the expression as four groups of 2/5.
  2. Write repeated addition: 2/5 + 2/5 + 2/5 + 2/5.
  3. Add the fifths: 8/5.
  4. Convert if useful: 8/5 = 1 3/5.
Answer: 8/5, or 1 3/5

Example 2 · A fraction of a quantity

Find 3/4 of 20.

  1. The denominator 4 tells us to split 20 into 4 equal parts.
  2. 20 ÷ 4 = 5, so one fourth of 20 is 5.
  3. The numerator 3 tells us to take 3 of those parts.
  4. 3 × 5 = 15.
Answer: 15
Ready for the next level? Open Examples 3–6

Example 3 · Predict the product type

Predict, then find 7 × 3/8.

  1. The fractional factor 3/8 is less than 1, so the product should be less than 7.
  2. Multiply the numerator by the natural number: 7 × 3 = 21.
  3. Keep the eighth-sized unit: 21/8.
  4. 21/8 = 2 5/8, which is less than 7 as predicted.
Answer: 21/8, or 2 5/8

Example 4 · Unit fraction and division

Explain why 1/6 × 30 = 30 ÷ 6.

  1. One sixth of a quantity means split the quantity into 6 equal parts.
  2. 30 ÷ 6 = 5.
  3. So one sixth of 30 is 5.
  4. Therefore 1/6 × 30 and 30 ÷ 6 have the same value.
Answer: 5

Example 5 · Improper fractional factor

Will 5 × 3/2 be less than or greater than 5? Then calculate.

  1. 3/2 is greater than 1, so the product should be greater than 5.
  2. 5 × 3/2 = 15/2.
  3. 15/2 = 7 1/2.
  4. 7 1/2 is greater than 5, so the prediction was reasonable.
Answer: 15/2, or 7 1/2

Example 6 · Multi-step context

A club has 30 stickers. It gives 2/5 of them away, then receives 8 new stickers. How many stickers does it have now?

  1. Write one expression: 30 − (2/5 × 30) + 8.
  2. Find two-fifths of 30: 30 ÷ 5 = 6, then 2 × 6 = 12.
  3. Subtract the 12 stickers that were given away: 30 − 12 = 18.
  4. Add the 8 new stickers: 18 + 8 = 26.
Answer: 26 stickers

Counters: Groups Of vs Part Of

  1. Place 12 counters on your desk.
  2. Model 3 × 1/4 using four-part fraction strips or a number line. Describe it as three groups of one fourth.
  3. Now model 1/4 of 12 counters by splitting the 12 counters into 4 equal groups and keeping 1 group.
  4. Choose a new example such as 2/3 of 12 or 4 × 2/3 and build a model.

REAL-LIFE CHALLENGE

Your class is preparing a community breakfast. One tray uses 3/4 cup of oats. You need 6 trays. You also have 40 fruit cups, and 3/5 of them will be placed on the breakfast tables while the rest are saved for later.

  1. Find the total amount of oats needed for 6 trays. Show the multiplication as repeated addition or groups.
  2. Find 3/5 of 40 fruit cups. Show how the denominator and numerator guide your steps.
  3. For each calculation, state whether your model is groups-of or part-of.
  4. Before calculating, explain why 3/5 of 40 must be less than 40.
  5. Write one sentence connecting a unit fraction such as 1/5 of 40 to division.
Check the core ideas

Oats: 6 × 3/4 = 18/4 = 9/2 = 4 1/2 cups. Fruit cups: 3/5 × 40 = 24 because 40 ÷ 5 = 8 and 3 × 8 = 24. The oats calculation naturally shows repeated groups; the fruit calculation naturally shows a fraction of a quantity. One fifth of 40 equals 40 ÷ 5.

WRAP UP

Ready to move on?

LESSON SUMMARY

  • A natural number times a fraction can be understood as repeated addition.
  • A fraction times a natural number can represent a fraction of a quantity.
  • The denominator names the size of the fractional units and does not change just because more groups are added.
  • A proper fractional factor can make a positive quantity smaller.
  • Multiplying by a unit fraction is equivalent to dividing by its denominator.
  • Grade 6 focuses on fraction–natural-number multiplication, not a general fraction × fraction algorithm.

IF YOU NEED SUPPORT

  • Rewrite natural × fraction multiplication as repeated addition.
  • For “fraction of” questions, find one unit fraction first by dividing.
  • Use a number line, counters, or fraction strips before writing the symbolic calculation.
  • Make a less/equal/greater prediction before you calculate.

IF YOU WANT A CHALLENGE

  • Create two different word problems for 4 × 3/5 and 3/5 × 4. Explain why the diagrams would look different even though the products match.
  • Find three different natural numbers that give a natural-number product when multiplied by 3/4. Explain the pattern.
  • Write a multi-step expression involving a fraction of a quantity, addition, and parentheses, then solve it.

PAT-STYLE THINKING

A student says 8 × 3/4 must be greater than 8 because multiplication always increases a number. Which explanation best corrects the student?

Show answer

Three-fourths is less than 1, so 3/4 of 8 is only part of 8. Split 8 into 4 equal groups of 2 and take 3 groups: 6. Multiplication by a proper fraction can make a positive quantity smaller.
