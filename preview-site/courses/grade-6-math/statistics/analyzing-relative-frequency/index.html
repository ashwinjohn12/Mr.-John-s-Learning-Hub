LESSON 9.3 · GRADE 6 MATH

Analyzing Relative Frequency

Use sample size and relative frequency together to judge how much confidence the data deserves.

Start with thisMake a prediction. You are not expected to know the answer yet.

START HERE

The big idea

Relative frequency varies from sample to sample, but larger samples usually give more reliable estimates of what tends to happen.

Experiments involve random variation, so two groups can get different relative frequencies even when they use the same fair setup. Relative frequency allows fair comparison across different sample sizes. As the number of trials grows, short-run irregularities have less influence and the data generally gives a better estimate of expected likelihood. This is the Grade 6 meaning of the law of large numbers.

LEARN

Build your understanding

01

Relative frequency lets different sample sizes be compared

Raw counts cannot be compared fairly when totals differ. Convert each count to a relative frequency before judging the pattern.

Remember: Compare part-to-whole, not count-to-count.

02

Different samples can produce different results

Random variation means two groups using the same experiment may get different relative frequencies.

Remember: Variation is normal; disagreement does not automatically mean someone made a mistake.

03

More trials usually strengthen an estimate

A larger sample gives random highs and lows more chances to balance out, so its relative frequency generally provides a better estimate of expected likelihood.

Remember: More data usually means more confidence, not perfect certainty.

04

The law of large numbers describes a long-run pattern

As more trials are performed, experimental relative frequency tends to become a better estimate of the expected likelihood for the experiment.

Remember: It does not promise that every larger sample is closer every single time.

05

Predictions inherit the quality of the data

If two samples suggest different predictions, the one based on more relevant data is usually more dependable.

Remember: Ask: How much data supports this prediction?

Careful conclusions

Fairness claims need evidence and careful language

A large imbalance in a large sample can be evidence that a supposedly fair setup deserves investigation, but experimental data should support a conclusion rather than be treated as absolute proof.

Remember: Use phrases such as “the data suggests” or “this is more consistent with.”

STATISTICS SENTENCE STARTER

The data suggests…

Instead of: “The spinner is unfair.”

Try: “The data suggests the spinner may be unfair because…”

Instead of: “Red will happen 25 times.”

Try: “Based on the data, we would expect about 25 red outcomes.”

Statistics describes evidence and uncertainty. Strong conclusions avoid words such as “proves,” “always,” and “guaranteed” unless the mathematics truly guarantees the result.

COMMON MIX-UPS

“The larger sample must always have the closer percentage.”

Larger samples are generally more reliable, but random variation can still make an individual larger sample less close on a particular run.

“If two samples disagree, one experiment is wrong.”

Random samples naturally vary. Compare the relative frequencies and sample sizes before drawing conclusions.

“The same percentage means the same amount of evidence.”

90% from 10 trials and 90% from 1000 trials have the same relative frequency but very different sample sizes and confidence.

“Experimental data proves the next outcome.”

Relative frequency estimates likelihood. It cannot guarantee the result of the next random trial.

WATCH THE THINKING

Worked examples

Start with the two core examples. Open Examples 3 and 4 when you are ready for a deeper connection.

Example 1 · Same pattern, different totals

Class A: 12 of 24 choose the Science Centre. Class B: 24 of 48 choose it. Compare.

  1. Class A: 12/24=50%.
  2. Class B: 24/48=50%.
  3. The raw counts differ, but the relative frequencies match.
Answer: Both groups show 50%.

Example 2 · Which prediction has stronger support?

Group A gets blue 5 times in 10 spins. Group B gets blue 15 times in 50 spins. Which sample should usually guide a future prediction?

  1. Group A: 5/10=50%.
  2. Group B: 15/50=30%.
  3. The estimates differ.
  4. Group B has five times as many trials, so its estimate is generally more reliable.
Answer: Use Group B as the stronger evidence.
Ready for more? Open Examples 3 and 4

Example 3 · Combine samples

Ava gets six 12 times in 40 rolls. Jamal gets six 20 times in 80 rolls. What is the combined relative frequency?

  1. Combine frequencies: 12+20=32.
  2. Combine trials: 40+80=120.
  3. 32/120≈0.267.
  4. Convert to about 26.7%.
Answer: About 26.7%.

Example 4 · Fairness from a larger sample

A four-colour equal spinner is spun 240 times: red 58, blue 61, green 64, yellow 57. Is the data consistent with a fair spinner?

  1. A fair four-colour spinner suggests about 25% per colour.
  2. The four counts are all close to 60 out of 240.
  3. The relative frequencies are close to one another.
Answer: Yes. The data is reasonably consistent with equal likelihood.

LESSON SUMMARY

  • Relative frequency makes different sample sizes comparable.
  • Random samples naturally vary.
  • Larger samples generally support more reliable estimates.
  • The law of large numbers describes improved long-run estimation, not perfect short-run balance.
  • Predictions should be supported by data and stated carefully.
Need more help?
  • Write each sample as “frequency out of total” before comparing.
  • Convert to percentages when the sample sizes differ.
  • After comparing percentages, ask which sample has more trials.
Want an extra challenge?
  • Simulate or collect 10,50,100, and 500 trials and graph how one relative frequency changes.
  • Find two samples with the same relative frequency but very different sample sizes and compare the evidence.
  • Explore the Canadian Space Agency moon-data connection from the resource by comparing proportions across different planetary data sets.

PAT-STYLE THINKING

Sample A has 8 heads in 10 tosses. Sample B has 51 heads in 100 tosses. Luis says Sample A is better evidence because 80% is farther from 50% and therefore more noticeable. Explain which sample should usually support the stronger estimate of a fair coin’s head likelihood.

Show a strong answer

Sample B should usually support the stronger estimate because it is based on 100 trials instead of 10. Sample A’s 80% can be caused by random variation in a very small sample. The larger sample gives more data and is closer to the expected 50%.
