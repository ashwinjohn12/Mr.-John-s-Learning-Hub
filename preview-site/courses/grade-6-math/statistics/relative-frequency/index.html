START HERE

The big idea

Relative frequency tells how often an outcome occurred compared with the total number of observations or trials.

Frequency is a count. Relative frequency is a part-to-whole comparison: category frequency ÷ total frequency. The same relationship can be written as a ratio, fraction, decimal, or percent. Because every category belongs to one whole data set, all relative frequencies together should total 1, or 100%. Relative frequency also lets us compare groups of different sizes and make evidence-based predictions.

1Find the countFrequency for the category.
2Compare to totalPart ÷ whole.
3Represent & useFraction ↔ decimal ↔ percent.
Think about itWhich shows stronger support for an outcome: 12 out of 20 or 20 out of 40? Explain without using only the raw counts.

LEARN

Build your understanding

01

Frequency is a count; relative frequency compares to the whole

A count by itself can be misleading when totals differ. Relative frequency compares a category count with the total number of observations or trials.

Remember: relative frequency = frequency ÷ total

02

“Out of” is part-to-whole language

If 15 of 50 people choose cats, the relative frequency is 15 out of 50. That can be written 15/50 and simplified.

Remember: The denominator is the total sample size.

03

One relationship can have several number forms

A relative frequency can be written as a fraction, decimal, or percent. For example, 10/40 = 0.25 = 25%.

Remember: Different forms, same part of the whole.

04

All categories make one whole

If every result belongs to exactly one category, the relative frequencies should add to 1 as fractions/decimals or 100% as percentages.

Remember: Use the total as a built-in error check.

05

Relative frequency makes comparisons fairer

Comparing 20 students in one class with 40 in another requires attention to the total. Percentages or equivalent part-to-whole comparisons reveal whether the patterns really match.

Remember: Compare proportions, not just counts.

Take the idea further

Past data can support a prediction

A relative frequency can be scaled to a new total to estimate how often an outcome might occur in a similar future sample.

Remember: A prediction is an estimate, not a guarantee.

PART → WHOLE

10 out of 40=10/40=0.25=25%

Every form describes the same relative frequency.

COMMON MIX-UPS

“The bigger frequency is always more important.”

A bigger count may come from a much bigger group. Compare each count with its total.

“The denominator is the number in the category.”

The numerator is the category frequency; the denominator is the total number of trials or responses.

“Fraction, decimal, and percent are different results.”

They can be equivalent representations of the same relative frequency.

“A prediction tells exactly what will happen.”

Relative frequency supports an estimate based on past data. Future samples can still vary.

WATCH THE THINKING

Worked examples

Start with the two core examples. Open Examples 3 and 4 when you are ready for a deeper connection.

Example 1 · Count to relative frequency

In a survey of 50 people, 20 prefer dogs. Express the relative frequency.

  1. Frequency for dogs = 20.
  2. Total responses = 50.
  3. Write 20/50 and simplify to 2/5.
  4. 2÷5=0.4, which is 40%.
Answer: 20/50 = 2/5 = 0.40 = 40%.

Example 2 · Complete a data set

Card suits appear 6, 8, 10, and 16 times in 40 draws. What percentage belongs to the 10-card category?

  1. Use category frequency 10 and total 40.
  2. 10/40 simplifies to 1/4.
  3. 1/4=0.25=25%.
Answer: 25%.
Ready for more? Open Examples 3 and 4

Example 3 · Check the whole

Four categories have relative frequencies 50%, 25%, 15%, and 10%. Is the table complete?

  1. Add the percentages.
  2. 50+25+15+10=100.
  3. A complete set of exclusive categories should total 100%.
Answer: Yes. The relative frequencies total 100%.

Example 4 · Predict from data

5 out of 20 students prefer grapes. About how many out of 80 might prefer grapes in a similar group?

  1. 5/20 = 25%.
  2. 80 is four times 20.
  3. Scale 5 by the same factor: 5×4=20.
Answer: About 20 students.

LESSON SUMMARY

  • Relative frequency compares a category count with the total.
  • It can be written as a fraction, decimal, or percent.
  • All categories in a complete data set should total 1 or 100%.
  • Relative frequency helps compare groups with different totals.
  • Relative frequency can support predictions for similar future samples.
Need more help?
  • Write “category count / total” before converting.
  • Use a 100-grid to connect decimals and percentages visually.
  • Add all category percentages to check for 100%.
Want an extra challenge?
  • Create two different-sized data sets with the same relative frequencies.
  • Design a table with four categories whose percentages total 100% and convert them to decimals.
  • Explain why a prediction based on relative frequency is reasonable but not guaranteed.

PAT-STYLE THINKING

Class A has 15 students choose basketball out of 30. Class B has 24 students choose basketball out of 48. Mia says Class B prefers basketball more because 24 is greater than 15. Explain the error.

Show a strong answer

Mia compared frequencies without considering the different totals. Class A has 15/30=50% and Class B has 24/48=50%, so the relative frequency is the same.
