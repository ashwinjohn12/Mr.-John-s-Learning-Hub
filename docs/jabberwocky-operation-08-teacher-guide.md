# Jabberwocky Phase 1 — Operation 08 Teacher Guide

## JCEC Ecological Report

### Purpose
Operation 08 is the culminating synthesis for **Interactions & Ecosystems / Mission Phase 1**. Students revisit evidence from Operations 02–07, design a protected-area/human-footprint plan, assign a final Phase 1 ecological status, and leave a usable Continental Archive handoff.

The report answers an ecological question only:

**What does current ecological evidence say humans may safely do on this continent, and what must they protect?**

It is intentionally **not** the final year-long colonization decision. Plants/resources, thermal systems, structures, and planetary geology remain future scientific divisions.

## Alberta Grade 7 Science alignment
The final report gives students a structured opportunity to synthesize evidence about:
- biotic and abiotic ecosystem components;
- habitat and organism needs;
- producer/consumer/decomposer relationships;
- food webs, competition, symbiosis, and interdependence;
- population monitoring and environmental change;
- succession, recovery, resilience, and reference sites;
- introduced species and biosecurity;
- pollutants and contaminant movement through food webs;
- intended and unintended human impacts;
- environmental monitoring and uncertainty;
- evidence-based environmental decision-making;
- actions that avoid, reduce, monitor, or restore ecological impacts.

## Recommended timing
### Class 1 — Evidence audit and map planning (45–60 min)
1. Reopen Continental Archive artifacts — 10 min
2. Complete Operation 02–07 evidence matrix — 15–20 min
3. Select four strongest pieces of evidence — 10 min
4. Begin protected-area plan — 15–20 min

### Class 2 — Ecological report (50–70 min)
1. Complete protected-area decisions and human rules — 10–15 min
2. Choose working GREEN/YELLOW/ORANGE/RED status — 5 min
3. Build final evidence/reasoning — 20–30 min
4. Quality check and revise — 10–15 min

### Class 3 — Presentation / archive handoff (optional but recommended)
Use for short team briefings, peer critique, final report submission, and individual reflection.

## Phase 1 status definitions
### GREEN
Ecological evidence supports continued exploration with normal protection rules and monitoring.

### YELLOW
Continued exploration may proceed only with specific restrictions, protected areas, and monitoring.

### ORANGE
Serious ecological risks remain unresolved. Humans should be restricted mainly to essential research, containment, or restoration work.

### RED
Current evidence supports pausing expansion because ecological harm or uncertainty is too great.

There is **no predetermined correct colour for a continent**. Different teams can defend different ratings if they accurately use evidence and acknowledge trade-offs/uncertainty.

## Important framing
Avoid letting students interpret the status as:
- “good continent / bad continent”;
- final approval for a city;
- a ranking of which ecosystem is valuable;
- a prediction that future science will not change the decision.

The status is an **ecological clearance under current evidence**.

## Evidence audit
Students revisit one important finding from each operation:
- **02 Environmental Survey:** biotic/abiotic conditions and landing-site profile;
- **03 First Contact:** native organism adaptations and provisional niche;
- **04 Ecosystem Network:** food web, competition/symbiosis, interdependence;
- **05 Population Investigation:** monitoring trends, limiting factors, distribution/detectability, uncertainty;
- **06 Environmental Change:** succession, resilience, reference comparison, recovery;
- **07 Classified Incident:** introduced species, contamination, unintended human effects.

Final recommendation requirement:
- evidence from **at least four different operations**;
- at least one quantitative piece of evidence;
- explanation of why each selected piece matters.

This prevents a report built entirely around the most recent dramatic incident.

## Protected-area planning
The website gives each continent four conceptual planning zones. These are intentionally **not exact geographic maps**; students are creating a scientific planning model from the evidence currently available.

Students assign each zone one access category:
- **PROTECT** — no routine human access;
- **RESEARCH ONLY** — controlled scientific access;
- **LIMITED ACCESS** — restricted human use with monitoring;
- **OPERATIONS** — concentrated human activity under strict rules.

### Teacher prompt
Ask students to defend boundaries and access using questions such as:
- What ecological feature are you protecting?
- What human pathway caused or could cause harm here?
- Where should monitoring occur?
- Can an operations zone be placed where damage is already concentrated rather than fragmenting a new habitat?
- What would make you change this access level later?

## Human rules
Every team writes at least three rules:
1. a biosecurity/spread-prevention rule;
2. a habitat-disturbance reduction rule;
3. a monitoring-before-expansion rule.

Strong rules should be specific and measurable when possible.

Weak: “Be careful with nature.”

Stronger: “All rover wheels and cargo mats must be inspected and cleaned before moving from the operations zone into protected habitat, and new introduced-species detections trigger a halt to route expansion.”

## Continent-specific synthesis notes
These are not answer keys.

### Gyre
Likely strengths: fresh water, productive valleys, connected habitats.
Likely concerns: fragile alpine recovery, erosion, moisture limitation, Ryegrass spread, X-17 pathway.
A YELLOW rating may be common, but GREEN with strong restrictions or ORANGE under a precautionary argument can be defensible.

### Brillig
Likely strengths: high productivity, water, biodiversity.
Likely concerns: clearing/erosion, incomplete rainforest structural recovery, introduced soil organisms, drainage contamination.
Students should not equate rapid greening with resilience of the whole rainforest system.

### Manxome
Likely strengths: mild climate and productive coastal webs.
Likely concerns: storm disturbance, vulnerable feeding rocks, island biosecurity, shoreline contamination.
Transport between islands should matter in protection planning.

### Slithy Toves
Likely strengths: specialized adaptations and functioning desert food webs.
Likely concerns: severe water limitation, slow soil-crust recovery, human water subsidies favouring an introduced plant, spring contamination.
A cautious status is reasonable because recovery can be extremely slow.

### Wabe
Likely strengths: functioning Arctic adaptations and seasonal food web.
Likely concerns: slow recovery, compaction/drainage, short productivity season, disturbed-ground Coldgrass.
Students should consider whether reducing the disturbed habitat is as important as removing the introduced species.

### Bandersnatch
Likely strengths: prairie resilience, deep roots, strong recovery of cover.
Likely concerns: richness lag, water-linked animal movement, rapid Brome spread through connected prairie.
Its original “Excellent” preliminary rating is intentionally challengeable.

### Gimble
Likely strengths: intact forest seed/spore/litter sources and complex food web.
Likely concerns: slow rebuilding of forest-floor complexity, dependence on mature bark habitat, introduced bark insect, runoff contaminant.
Avoid treating tree cover alone as evidence the forest system is unaffected.

### Mimsy
Likely strengths: wetland recolonization and specialized shallow-water food web.
Likely concerns: hydrology sensitivity, soft-ground disturbance, Earth Marsh Grass spread, contaminant pathway.
Its original “Poor” preliminary rating is intentionally challengeable: wetlands may be ecologically valuable even if conventional human development is difficult.

## Full report structure
The student page asks teams to include:
1. **Ecosystem profile** — important biotic/abiotic conditions, focal species, niche;
2. **Interdependencies** — food web, competition/symbiosis, key relationship;
3. **Change & resilience** — population, disturbance, succession, monitoring;
4. **Human-impact evidence** — introduced species, contaminant, or another human-caused impact;
5. **Protection plan** — zones + three human rules;
6. **Final recommendation** — ecological status, strongest evidence, uncertainty.

## Submission architecture
This operation is a **major graded artifact**, so the established Jabberwocky workflow is:
- website = mission control / evidence / planning;
- full report, map, presentation, or major artifact = submit where the teacher directs, typically Google Classroom;
- Master JCEC Form = concise executive archive summary for the teacher dashboard and future archive continuity.

### JCEC executive summary
Use:
- **Submission Type:** Continental Archive
- **Mission Phase:** 01 First Contact
- **Operation / Task:** JCEC Ecological Report — Phase 1 Final Recommendation
- **Continent:** assigned continent
- **Team ID:** teacher assigned
- **Team Members:** everyone represented

Form mapping:
- **Claim / Decision / Main Finding:** ecological status + clearance recommendation;
- **Evidence:** at least four strong pieces across multiple operations;
- **Reasoning / Explanation:** why evidence supports status + major trade-offs + protection rules + uncertainty;
- **Pathfinder Reflection:** blank for team archive unless directed.

## Archive handoff checklist
The Continental Archive should contain or summarize:
- landing-site profile;
- native organism profile;
- Food Web V1;
- population-case finding;
- recovery-zone recommendation;
- biosecurity/contamination finding;
- final protected-area plan;
- final ecological status;
- three human rules;
- remaining uncertainty/questions.

The next team should be able to understand the ecological situation without redoing Phase 1.

## Individual reflection
Students compare their Operation 01 starting hypothesis with the Phase 1 evidence.

Good reflection indicators:
- names the initial assessment accurately;
- identifies evidence that changed or sharpened thinking;
- recognizes that changing a conclusion when evidence changes is good scientific practice;
- distinguishes ecological recommendation from final settlement decision;
- identifies a useful future research question.

## Suggested assessment dimensions
### Science Understanding
Accurate ecological concepts, relationships, and interpretation.

### Evidence
Relevant evidence across multiple operations, including quantitative evidence.

### Reasoning
Clear links between evidence, ecological mechanisms, protection plan, and final status.

### Communication / Decision-Making
Clear report, defensible trade-offs, uncertainty, and usable handoff.

A four-level rubric can use the same recurring dimensions used throughout the project for continuity.

## Common misconceptions
### “The preliminary rating tells us the final rating.”
It was intentionally based on limited arrival data.

### “If our rating changed, our earlier work was wrong.”
The purpose of the year-long story is revision with better evidence.

### “A protected zone means humans can never enter.”
The access categories distinguish routine use, controlled research, limited access, and operations.

### “The final report should include every fact we learned.”
Students should select the strongest evidence and explain why it matters.

### “Phase 1 decides whether humans colonize Jabberwocky.”
No. It supplies the ecological evidence layer for the later final decision.

## Closing the phase
End with the archive handoff idea:

**“You are not leaving a completed answer. You are leaving the best ecological evidence currently available.”**

Then rotate teams/continents when the next unit begins. The new group should inherit the archive rather than starting over.
