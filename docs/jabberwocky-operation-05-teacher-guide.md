# Jabberwocky Phase 1 — Operation 05 Teacher Guide

## Population Investigation

### Purpose
Operation 05 moves students from **predicting change from a food web** to **interpreting actual population evidence over time**. Students first complete a hands-on population-sampling investigation, then analyze three continent-specific datasets plus supporting field evidence and construct a Claim–Evidence–Reasoning (CER) explanation.

The central scientific message is:

**A changing survey count is evidence, but it is not automatically the same thing as a changing total population.**

Students must consider sampling, distribution, resource availability, abiotic conditions, movement, interactions, and uncertainty before selecting an explanation.

## Alberta Grade 7 Science alignment
This operation supports Interactions and Ecosystems work by having students:
- monitor and interpret changes in populations and environmental conditions;
- use quantitative and qualitative evidence from ecological monitoring;
- relate population patterns to resources, abiotic conditions, habitat, and ecological interactions;
- recognize that organism distribution can change even when total population size is not known;
- identify limiting factors and plausible causes of population change;
- compare multiple evidence sources rather than relying on a single observation;
- distinguish correlation from a fully established cause-and-effect conclusion;
- identify limitations in data and monitoring methods;
- make evidence-based explanations and identify additional evidence needed.

This operation intentionally stays with **natural ecological variation and monitoring uncertainty**. Succession/recovery is held for Operation 06, and the later biosecurity, pollutant, and human-impact storylines remain unrevealed.

## Recommended timing
### Class 1 — Population Sampling Lab (45–60 min)
1. Mission briefing and population vocabulary — 8–10 min
2. Team roles and habitat-grid setup — 5 min
3. Round 1 random sampling — 10–15 min
4. Reveal true population and calculate error — 5–8 min
5. Round 2 clustered distribution — 10–15 min
6. Debrief sampling, distribution, and repeated monitoring — 8–10 min

### Class 2 — Continental Population Case (45–60 min)
1. Open assigned case and read the mystery — 5 min
2. Read all three graphs and the data table — 12–15 min
3. Examine supporting evidence — 8–10 min
4. Compare possible claims — 5–8 min
5. Build CER on the paper case record — 15–20 min

### Optional Class 3 — CER critique / archive (30–50 min)
Use if you want students to:
- exchange CERs with another team for evidence checking;
- revise overclaims such as “the graph proves”;
- compare how different continents require different interpretations;
- submit the official archive record and individual reflections with less time pressure.

## Population Sampling Lab
### Materials per team
- one printed 5 × 5 habitat grid;
- one opaque cup containing an **unknown** number of small counters/beans/beads/paper dots;
- pencil;
- calculator if desired;
- Operation 05 Population Sampling Record.

Recommended: prepare cups with different totals, roughly 30–50 counters. Students should not know the total before Round 1.

### Round 1
Students:
1. pour the unknown counters onto the 25-cell grid without counting the total;
2. if a counter lands on a line, move it gently into the square containing most of the counter;
3. use the website to generate five unique Round 1 quadrats;
4. record those numbers before sampling;
5. count only those quadrats;
6. calculate mean count per quadrat;
7. multiply the mean by 25 to estimate total population;
8. finally count every counter to reveal the true total.

The website now preserves the Round 1 quadrat numbers on screen instead of replacing them when students begin Round 2.

The goal is not mathematical precision. The goal is to establish that an estimate can differ from a true count even when the method is reasonable.

### Round 2
Students keep the **same total population** but move many counters into a cluster of neighbouring quadrats. Only after the distribution has changed should they generate the Round 2 sample.

The Round 2 button unlocks after Round 1 has been generated, and both sample sets remain visible for comparison. A reset button is provided only for restarting the activity if needed.

Key discussion:
- total population did not change;
- distribution changed;
- a small random sample may now produce a very different estimate;
- repeated sampling and adequate sample size improve confidence.

Do not frame one team’s estimate as “bad science” simply because it is inaccurate. Sampling uncertainty is the learning target.

## Population monitoring concepts
### Detections are not automatically total population
A prominent student-facing reminder now appears immediately before the continent case data:

**DETECTIONS ≠ TOTAL POPULATION**

A detection count can change because:
- population size changed;
- organisms moved;
- behaviour or activity changed;
- habitat conditions changed where organisms were found;
- or the monitoring method detected them differently.

The continental graphs deliberately use terms such as **detections**, **activity signs**, or **standardized survey counts**. Students should not automatically convert these into exact total population size.

Ask:
- What exactly was measured?
- Was the same method used each week?
- Could behaviour or distribution change detection?
- Do we have evidence from another location or another monitoring method?

### Graph-scale warning
The three graphs in a case can use different y-axis scales. Students now see a **Graph Detective Rule** before graph analysis:

**Do not compare line steepness until you check the y-axis values.**

A line that looks steep may represent a smaller numerical change than a line on another graph. Require students to use exact values or clearly described numerical changes in their CER.

### Correlation and causation
At this grade level, the goal is not formal statistical analysis. Students should understand that:
- patterns changing together can support an explanation;
- timing matters;
- a plausible biological mechanism strengthens the explanation;
- independent evidence strengthens the explanation;
- alternative explanations should be considered;
- strong language such as **proves** is usually not justified by these datasets.

Useful student wording:
- supports;
- suggests;
- is consistent with;
- likely contributed to;
- may explain;
- current evidence does not support.

## Team roles for the continental case
- **Data Reader** — reads axes, units, exact values, and trends aloud.
- **Evidence Recorder** — records precise data values and field observations.
- **JCEC Operator** — uses interactive controls only after discussion.
- **Alternative-Explanation Checker** — challenges overclaims and asks what else could explain the pattern.

If teams have fewer than four students, combine roles.

## Evidence selection protocol
Students should **read all supporting evidence records before marking key evidence**. The point is not to select three cards merely because three are required.

Ask students to classify the usefulness of evidence mentally or orally:
- Does it support the working claim?
- Does it weaken another explanation?
- Does it help rule out a cause?
- Does it introduce uncertainty?

Their final CER should still use graph/table values as well as field observations.

## Working-claim board
All three claims are now written as **plausible scientific hypotheses**, rather than one obviously correct answer and two obviously unreasonable choices.

The strongest-supported claim is deliberately placed in different letter positions across continents. Students should not be able to infer a pattern such as “Claim A is always best.”

Recommended teacher move:
Before allowing a team to select a working claim, ask:

**“What evidence supports each claim, and what evidence creates a problem for it?”**

The website gives no correctness feedback. Students may revise their working claim.

## CER expectations
### Claim
A concise explanation that does not exceed what the evidence can support.

### Evidence
At least three specific pieces of evidence, including:
- exact graph/table values or changes;
- at least two different datasets;
- ideally one supporting field observation.

Weak: “The grass went down.”

Stronger: “Bluecrest Grass cover fell from 69% in Week 3 to 31% in Week 8 while Ridgeback detections fell from 44 to 25 over the same period.”

### Reasoning
Students explain why the evidence connects to the claim using ecological science: food resources, limiting factors, habitat, activity patterns, movement, or trophic relationships.

The student page now includes a short **non-Jabberwocky CER model** using a schoolyard microhabitat example. Its purpose is to distinguish:
- **Evidence = what was observed or measured**
- **Reasoning = why that evidence supports the claim**

Do not use the example as a sentence frame students must copy word-for-word.

A strong CER also names:
- one alternative explanation;
- why it is currently weaker OR what evidence would be needed to test it;
- one uncertainty or next measurement.

## Continental interpretation guide
These are intended interpretations, not scripts students must reproduce. Because several claims can be partly reasonable, reward the quality of evidence and cautious reasoning rather than exact wording.

### Gyre — Ridgeback Grazer
Core pattern:
- Ridgeback detections: 44 → 25 from Week 3 to Week 8;
- Bluecrest Grass cover: 69% → 31%;
- soil moisture: 43% → low 20s;
- Crag Hunter detections remain stable;
- sensors pass checks;
- tracks become concentrated near remaining green valley floor.

Best-supported interpretation:
A combined resource-and-distribution explanation is strongest. Reduced moisture is associated with reduced Bluecrest Grass, which likely affected food availability, while tracks indicate Ridgebacks also became concentrated near greener habitat. The current transect data do not cleanly separate true population decline from redistribution.

Strong student answers may argue for either food limitation or redistribution if they explicitly identify what the present evidence cannot determine.

### Brillig — Canopy Glider
Core pattern:
- severe rainfall/storm begins Week 4;
- ripe Glowfruit index falls from 82 to 25 by Week 7;
- Canopy Glider detections fall after the resource decline;
- fruiting branches are visibly damaged;
- Vine Stalker detections do not increase;
- shelter cavities mostly remain intact.

Best-supported interpretation:
Storm damage reduced a major food resource, and lower Canopy Glider detections followed that loss. Shelter loss is less supported because most monitored cavities remained intact.

A broader behaviour/distribution response can be discussed, but food-resource evidence is stronger.

### Manxome — Tide Skipper
Core pattern:
- high waves in Weeks 4–5;
- Tidefilm Algae cover drops sharply;
- Tide Skipper detections fall at the exposed site;
- both begin to recover as wave height falls;
- protected-cove counts change much less;
- predator detections do not rise.

Best-supported interpretation:
The strongest explanation combines habitat/resource disturbance with likely redistribution: high waves stripped Tidefilm Algae from exposed rocks and changed how usable the exposed shoreline was for Tide Skippers. The evidence does not establish that all missing Tide Skippers died.

### Slithy Toves — Dune Runner
Core pattern:
- midday detections fall from 18 to 4 during the hottest period;
- night-camera detections remain around 20–23;
- midday temperature rises to 47°C;
- burrows remain much cooler;
- fresh tracks shift to night;
- no unusual mortality signal.

Best-supported interpretation:
Dune Runners changed activity timing and/or use of cooler refuges. Midday survey counts fell, but current evidence does **not** support a comparable total-population crash.

This case is especially important for teaching **detectability**.

### Wabe — Snow Burrower
Core pattern:
- Snow Burrower activity signs decline;
- green Tundra Mat cover declines;
- snow persists over plant plots;
- food caches contain less fresh plant material;
- sampled body condition declines;
- predator detections remain low.

Best-supported interpretation:
Persistent snow delayed plant availability, reducing a food resource and likely contributing to lower activity and body condition.

A detectability explanation remains plausible, but it does not explain the declining fresh food in caches and lower sampled body condition as well as the food-limitation explanation does.

### Bandersnatch — Plains Strider
Core pattern:
- Jubjub-area detections fall from 70 to 20;
- East Waterhole detections rise from 12 to 72;
- local Jubjub-area surface water drops sharply;
- tracks increasingly connect the two areas;
- East Waterhole retains more water;
- no unusual mortality signal.

Best-supported interpretation:
Many Plains Striders changed distribution toward the more reliable water source. A local survey decline is not evidence of a continent-wide population crash.

The data cannot prove that no mortality occurred, so cautious teams may retain some uncertainty about total population size.

### Gimble — Barkclimber
Core pattern:
- overnight cold snap begins Week 4;
- Bark Grub index falls before/alongside Barkclimber decline;
- Barkclimbers spend longer searching trunks;
- shelter availability is stable;
- predator detections remain normal.

Best-supported interpretation:
The cold event was followed by reduced prey availability, which likely contributed to lower Barkclimber detections through the food web.

Important reasoning:
Students should distinguish an **indirect food-web pathway** from a possible direct cold effect. The evidence supports the prey pathway more strongly, but it does not prove temperature had no direct influence.

### Mimsy — Reedcrawler
Core pattern:
- Site A detections fall;
- Site B detections rise;
- water level rises;
- the shallow reed-water boundary physically shifts toward Site B;
- combined detections remain much more stable than either site alone;
- predators do not increase substantially.

Best-supported interpretation:
Rising water changed habitat location and Reedcrawler distribution. One-site decline is not evidence that Reedcrawlers disappeared from Mimsy.

## Cross-continent comparison opportunity
After teams submit, ask each continent for a 30-second summary using:

**“Our count changed because the best-supported explanation is ______. The most important evidence was ______. We are still uncertain about ______.”**

The class should notice that similar-looking declining graphs can have very different explanations:
- resource limitation;
- habitat disturbance;
- altered activity/detectability;
- redistribution/movement;
- indirect food-web effects.

This is a major conceptual payoff of Operation 05.

## Common misconceptions
### “Lower count = same-size population decline.”
Redirect to sampling, detectability, and distribution.

### “The steepest-looking graph changed the most.”
Redirect students to the y-axis values and units. Different graphs may use different scales.

### “Two lines match, so one caused the other.”
Ask for mechanism, timing, independent evidence, and alternatives.

### “One claim must be obviously correct.”
The claims are intentionally plausible. Students must identify which is **best supported**, not which is written to sound most scientific.

### “One weird data point changes the whole conclusion.”
Encourage students to look for overall patterns and repeated evidence unless the outlier has a known explanation.

### “Uncertainty means we failed.”
Uncertainty identifies what should be measured next and is part of strong scientific reasoning.

## Continental Archive submission
One student submits for the team through the JCEC Submission Portal.

Use:
- **Submission Type:** Continental Archive
- **Mission Phase:** 01 First Contact
- **Operation / Task:** Population Investigation — Population Case Finding
- **Continent:** assigned continent
- **Team ID:** teacher-assigned phase + continent + team number, e.g. `P1-GY-01`
- **Team Members:** everyone represented by the submission

Form mapping:
- **Claim / Decision / Main Finding:** concise best-supported population explanation;
- **Evidence:** at least three precise values/observations from two or more evidence sources;
- **Reasoning / Explanation:** mechanism + why evidence supports the claim + alternative/uncertainty;
- **Pathfinder Reflection:** leave blank for the team archive unless directed otherwise.

The paper Population Case Record remains part of the Continental Archive.

## Individual accountability
After submission, each student reflects on:
- the strongest specific piece of evidence;
- how survey count can differ from total population change;
- an alternative explanation considered;
- the next measurement they would request.

Keep reflections in the Pathfinder Log unless individual submission is needed for assessment.

## Assessment opportunities
Formative evidence can include:
- random-sampling procedure;
- correct population-estimate calculation;
- explanation of sampling uncertainty;
- accurate graph/table reading;
- use of exact values rather than vague descriptions;
- recognition that visual steepness cannot be compared without checking scale;
- distinction between count, distribution, and population size;
- quality of claim;
- integration of multiple evidence sources;
- ecological reasoning;
- appropriate uncertainty language.

Suggested recurring rubric dimensions:
- Science Understanding
- Evidence
- Reasoning
- Communication / Decision-Making
