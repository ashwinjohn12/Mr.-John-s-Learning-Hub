# Jabberwocky Phase 1 — Operation 06 Teacher Guide

## Environmental Change

### Purpose
Operation 06 moves students from short-term population change into **disturbance, succession, recovery, resilience, environmental monitoring, and evidence-based management**. Students first complete a hands-on surface-cover/erosion investigation, then analyze a 55-day recovery dataset for their assigned continent and decide how JCEC should respond.

The central scientific message is:

**Recovery is not the same as returning instantly—or exactly—to the previous ecosystem.**

Students should monitor several indicators over time and compare the disturbed area with a less-disturbed reference site before deciding whether recovery is occurring.

## Alberta Grade 7 Science alignment
This operation supports Interactions and Ecosystems by having students:
- identify ecosystem change following a disturbance;
- monitor changes in biotic and abiotic conditions over time;
- interpret succession and recolonization evidence;
- recognize that ecosystems can respond at different rates;
- compare disturbed and reference environments;
- examine how vegetation/surface cover can influence erosion and runoff;
- distinguish visible plant cover from broader ecological recovery;
- use species richness as one indicator of community rebuilding;
- analyze an unintended impact of human exploration activity;
- consider natural recovery, limited active restoration, and carefully controlled human use;
- make an evidence-based environmental-management recommendation;
- identify uncertainty and additional monitoring needs.

Operation 06 intentionally remains a **small, known JCEC disturbance**. Do not reveal the Operation 07 biosecurity/contamination incident yet.

## Hands-on lab: Surface Cover & Erosion
### Materials per team
- two shallow trays or containers;
- equal amounts of soil;
- dry leaves, grass clippings, shredded paper, mesh, or another safe cover material;
- two collection cups;
- equal measured water portions;
- books/blocks to create the same gentle slope;
- measuring cup;
- towels for spills;
- Operation 06 two-page field packet.

### Fair-test design
Students change **surface cover** while keeping the following as consistent as possible:
- amount/type of soil;
- tray dimensions;
- slope;
- volume of water;
- pour height;
- pour duration;
- collection duration.

For Tray B, students should cover approximately **75% of the soil surface using one loose layer** of the teacher-provided material. They should not pack the material deeply into the soil unless you specifically want to investigate that variable.

Students measure:
- runoff volume;
- turbidity using the common 0–5 scale;
- visible erosion/channeling/pooling.

Turbidity scale:
- 0 = clear
- 1 = slightly cloudy
- 2 = cloudy
- 3 = muddy
- 4 = very muddy
- 5 = extremely muddy / opaque

Do not present this as a complete model of ecological succession. It demonstrates one relevant mechanism: **surface cover can influence runoff and soil loss**.

### Safety / cleanup
- Keep water well away from student devices.
- Use shallow trays and modest water volumes.
- Clean spills promptly.
- Avoid potting soil containing fertilizers/dust if students are likely to handle it heavily; dampen very dry soil if needed to reduce dust.
- Wash hands after handling soil.

## Student science concepts
### Disturbance
A disturbance changes biotic and/or abiotic parts of an ecosystem. It does not automatically mean the ecosystem is permanently destroyed.

### Succession
The biological community changes over time after disturbance. In this operation, soil remains at all JCEC recovery zones, so the examples are **secondary succession** rather than primary succession.

The student page now uses a simplified sequence:

**Disturbance → soil/some organisms remain → pioneer organisms establish → habitat/resources change → more organisms establish → community keeps changing**

Emphasize that this is a thinking model, **not a rigid ladder**. Different ecosystems can follow different pathways and future disturbances can redirect succession.

### Pioneer organisms
Early colonizers can establish in disturbed conditions. Their presence is evidence that succession is occurring, but they do not necessarily recreate the previous community.

### Species richness
Species richness is the **number of different species found in an area**. It is deliberately separated from percent living cover. A site can have high cover but low richness if one or a few species dominate.

### Reference site
Students compare the recovery zone with a nearby less-disturbed site.

The student page now explicitly states:

**A reference site is a comparison, not a time machine.**

The recovery zone does not need to become an exact copy of the reference site because natural ecosystems vary.

### Recovery / resilience
Recovery can include:
- increasing living cover;
- decreasing exposed surface;
- increasing species richness;
- improved soil stability;
- restored water flow/drainage;
- return of habitat structure and ecological relationships.

A site can show clear recovery while still remaining different from the reference ecosystem.

### Surface percentages
Students may notice that **exposed surface + living cover does not always equal 100%**. This is intentional. Remaining area may include:
- rock;
- leaf litter;
- woody material;
- standing water;
- snow/ice;
- other non-living or unclassified surface features.

Do not let students treat the dataset as erroneous because the two listed percentages do not total 100.

One packet per team is enough.

## Team roles
- **Timeline Reader** — reads each survey chronologically and identifies exact changes.
- **Indicator Tracker** — tracks exposed surface, living cover, and species richness.
- **Reference Checker** — compares Day 55 with the reference site.
- **Decision Challenger** — asks whether the management recommendation is justified by enough evidence and whether an intervention could create new problems.

## Recovery Gap panel
The website now displays a visual Day 55 vs reference comparison for:
- living cover;
- species richness.

This is intended to reduce working-memory load and help students answer:

**“What still has not recovered?”**

Students should still use the full timeline and field evidence. The Recovery Gap does not replace the earlier data.

## Important analysis change
Do **not** ask students to decide which indicator “changed the most” by comparing raw numbers across unlike units.

Exposed surface and living cover are percentages, while species richness is a number of species. Those values are not directly comparable as though they use one common scale.

The student prompt is now:

**Describe how each indicator changed from Day 1 to Day 55. Which change gives your team the strongest evidence of recovery, and why?**

Assess the quality of the reasoning rather than whether students choose one predetermined indicator.

## Continental interpretation guide
These are intended interpretations, not required scripts.

### Gyre
- Living cover: 8% → 31%; reference 82%.
- Richness: 2 → 6; reference 11.
- Exposed surface remains 59% at Day 55.
- Erosion channels persist.

Interpretation: Recovery is occurring but slowly. Cold/short growing conditions and erosion make repeated disturbance risky. Limited stabilization of actively eroding channels plus protection is strongly defensible, but teams may argue for full natural recovery if they address erosion monitoring well.

### Brillig
- Living cover: 7% → 77%; reference 94%.
- Richness: 3 → 13; reference 18.
- Erosion decreases as cover returns.
- Mature understory/canopy structure remains absent.

Interpretation: Visually rapid greening does not equal full rainforest recovery. Natural succession with continued monitoring is strongly defensible; limited native understory restoration may also be justified if students explain why intervention is targeted rather than cosmetic.

### Manxome
- Living cover: 11% → 62%; reference 76%.
- Richness: 3 → 9; reference 12.
- Exposed centre recovers more slowly than sheltered edges.
- Wind and salt continue to influence establishment.

Interpretation: Recovery is substantial but uneven. A targeted treatment of the exposed centre is defensible, but protection and natural recovery also remain plausible if teams acknowledge the persistent wind/salt constraints.

### Slithy Toves
- Living cover: 2% → 9%; reference only 38% because desert cover is naturally low.
- Richness: 1 → 2; reference 7.
- Biological soil crust remains strongly damaged.

Explain **biological soil crust** as a living community on the desert surface that helps stabilize soil.

Interpretation: Low vegetation cover is normal in the desert, so students cannot judge recovery by comparing plant cover to a forest-like expectation. Full protection is highly defensible. A tiny restoration experiment may also be scientifically reasonable if students preserve an untreated comparison area and acknowledge uncertainty.

### Wabe
- Living cover: 6% → 13%; reference 64%.
- Richness: 2 → 3; reference 9.
- Compacted ruts alter drainage.

Interpretation: Recovery is slow and the abiotic disturbance persists. Heavy intervention could cause additional damage. Continued protection or a very small drainage correction, only if monitoring justifies it, are both credible choices.

### Bandersnatch
- Living cover: 10% → 78%; reference 88%.
- Richness: 4 → 12; reference 14.
- Surviving roots support rapid regrowth.

Interpretation: Strong recovery is occurring. Limited controlled access may be more defensible here than in the slower-recovering ecosystems, provided monitoring thresholds are used and vehicle traffic remains restricted.

### Gimble
- Living cover: 9% → 59%; reference 90%.
- Richness: 3 → 10; reference 16.
- Surrounding forest supplies seeds/spores/litter.

Interpretation: Secondary succession is clear, but forest-floor complexity is still rebuilding. Natural recolonization is strongly defensible; small additions of local litter or a limited native planting comparison could also be justified.

### Mimsy
- Living cover: 14% → 66%; reference 79%.
- Richness: 4 → 11; reference 13.
- Water flow remains altered at Day 55.

Use the phrase **water flow (hydrology)** when introducing the term.

Interpretation: Vegetation is recovering quickly, but wetland hydrology remains changed. Carefully restoring the shallow flow path is strongly defensible because wetland function depends on water movement, not only plant cover.

## Management-choice guidance
All three choices on the student page are now designed to be **plausible management approaches with trade-offs** rather than one obvious “bad” distractor.

Across the cases, the choices generally represent combinations of:
- continued protection / natural recovery;
- limited active restoration;
- carefully controlled access or use with continued monitoring.

The strongest-supported choice is **not intended to occupy one consistent letter position** and can vary by continent.

Look for:
- use of exact Day 1 → Day 55 changes;
- comparison to reference values;
- awareness of an indicator that remains impaired;
- avoidance of “it looks green, so it is fixed” reasoning;
- attention to unintended effects of intervention;
- acknowledgement of management trade-offs;
- a monitoring plan.

A student team can reasonably defend different management approaches if the evidence and trade-offs are handled well.

## Recovery Record expectations
Teams should answer:
1. How did each indicator change from Day 1 to Day 55, and which change is their strongest evidence of recovery?
2. Which indicator or field observation still differs from the reference site?
3. What evidence indicates succession?
4. Why does increased living cover not prove full restoration?
5. What should JCEC measure next?

## Individual reflection
Students answer independently:
- which indicator they trusted most;
- evidence that change occurred over time;
- evidence recovery is incomplete;
- whether they personally prefer natural recovery, active restoration, controlled use, or a combination.

The individual answer may differ from the team recommendation if it is evidence-based.

## Common misconceptions
### “More plants = fully recovered.”
Redirect to species richness, reference site, soil/water conditions, structure, and relationships.

### “The biggest number means the biggest ecological change.”
The indicators use different units. Students should interpret each indicator and explain which provides the strongest evidence rather than ranking unlike quantities mechanically.

### “Exposed surface + living cover must equal 100%.”
Remind students that rock, litter, water, woody material, snow, and other surface categories can occupy the remaining area.

### “Succession always ends at one exact final community.”
Avoid presenting succession as a rigid ladder. Conditions, future disturbances, and species interactions can change the path.

### “Humans should always fix a disturbed site immediately.”
Intervention itself can cause disturbance. Students should compare benefits, risks, and evidence.

### “Natural recovery means do nothing and stop monitoring.”
Protection and monitoring are active management choices.

### “Reference site = perfect copy of the past.”
Reference sites provide useful comparison but naturally vary too.

## Cross-continent discussion
A useful closing prompt is:

**“Our recovery zone is changing by ______, but it is still different from the reference site because ______. We recommend ______ because ______.”**

The class should notice that recovery speed differs dramatically among ecosystems and that the best indicator is not always plant cover.

## Assessment opportunities
Formative/summative evidence can include:
- fair-test procedure and variable control;
- runoff/turbidity observations;
- correct interpretation of succession;
- quantitative timeline comparison;
- use of reference-site evidence;
- distinction between recovery and exact restoration;
- management reasoning;
- uncertainty/monitoring plan;
- communication of trade-offs.

Suggested recurring rubric dimensions:
- Science Understanding
- Evidence
- Reasoning
- Communication / Decision-Making
