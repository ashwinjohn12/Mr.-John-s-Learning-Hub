# Jabberwocky Phase 1 — Operation 03 Teacher Guide

## First Contact

### Purpose
Operation 03 is the first direct investigation of a native Jabberwocky organism. It deliberately avoids a free-form “invent an alien” task. Students first complete a hands-on structure-and-function model lab, then use continent-specific observations to construct a defensible organism profile.

The central rule is **evidence first, imagination second**. A creative feature is only acceptable when students can connect it to observed evidence, an environmental condition, or clearly label it as an uncertainty/hypothesis.

## Alberta Grade 7 Science alignment
This operation supports the Interactions and Ecosystems work by having students:
- relate organism structures and behaviours to environmental conditions and survival needs;
- examine how organisms are suited to particular habitats;
- develop a provisional understanding of an organism’s niche through habitat, resource use, activity, and shelter;
- interpret indirect evidence such as tracks, feeding traces, thermal data, and behaviour records;
- distinguish observations from inferences;
- construct and revise a scientific model from evidence;
- communicate uncertainty instead of presenting unsupported assumptions as facts.

Operation 03 does **not** attempt to teach natural selection as a simulated process. The Survival Structures Lab models how structure affects function in different conditions. Students should not conclude that an organism simply develops a feature because it needs one.

The full food-web relationships of the native organism remain incomplete. Operation 04 will expand from one organism to the larger ecosystem network.

## Recommended timing
### Class 1 — Survival Structures Lab (45–60 min)
1. Mission briefing / adaptation, structure-function, and niche refresher — 8–10 min
2. Set up team roles and resource zones — 5 min
3. Timed tool trials — 20–25 min
4. Analyze results and identify trade-offs — 10–15 min
5. Connect lab evidence to adaptation reasoning — 5 min

### Class 2 — First Contact Reconstruction (45–60 min)
1. Revisit Operation 02 environmental profile — 5 min
2. Open continent-specific First Contact evidence — 10–15 min
3. Select key evidence and discuss inferences — 10 min
4. Complete Native Organism Reconstruction sheet — 15–20 min
5. Reveal JCEC provisional field name — 2 min
6. Begin archive wording — 5–10 min

### Optional Class 3 — Archive / critique / gallery (30–60 min)
Use if you want students to:
- compare reconstructions between teams;
- defend adaptations orally;
- revise weak evidence links;
- complete a more polished labelled field sketch;
- submit and reflect with less time pressure.

## Materials per team
### Survival Structures Lab
Minimum:
- plastic spoon
- tweezers
- clothespin or small tongs
- chopsticks
- timer
- collection cup
- three trays / resource zones
- several safe craft objects of different sizes/shapes

Suggested resource-zone materials:
- **Open Ground:** large pom-poms spread on a tray
- **Narrow Spaces:** small beads, paper clips, or other small objects in an ice-cube tray, egg carton, or shallow cups
- **Tangled Cover:** pipe-cleaner loops, rubber bands, or irregular craft pieces arranged around pencils, blocks, or simple barriers

Substitutions are encouraged. The scientific requirement is that students test the **same structures under several different conditions using consistent rules**.

### Reconstruction
- one printed Operation 03 packet per team
- pencil
- optional coloured pencils for labelled field sketch

## Fair-test guidance
Recommended trial rules:
- 20 seconds per structure per resource zone;
- one resource moved at a time;
- no fingers assisting the tool;
- reset the zone between tests;
- use the same collection cup and start position;
- record counts immediately.

A perfectly controlled experiment is not the goal, but students should recognize why keeping timing and rules consistent improves the comparison.

## Safety
Use normal classroom-safe tools only. Avoid sharp forceps, breakable containers, or very small objects if they present a concern in your setting. Students should not put lab materials in their mouths. Use non-food craft materials where allergies or food-use policies are a concern.

## Important conceptual debrief
The key conclusion is not “the spoon wins.” Students should recognize:
- different structures perform differently under different conditions;
- a structure may be highly effective for one task but weak for another;
- “best” only makes sense relative to a function and environment;
- trade-offs are scientifically useful evidence;
- this model helps students reason about organism structures but does not reproduce a real ecosystem or evolutionary process.

## First Contact workflow
Each team receives six pieces of indirect evidence for the continent stored from Operation 01. Evidence sources include tracks, feeding traces, body images, thermal data, movement logs, and shelter observations.

Students should:
1. read all six clues before drawing;
2. select at least three especially important clues;
3. distinguish what was observed from what is inferred;
4. reconstruct at least three structural adaptations and one behavioural adaptation;
5. describe a provisional niche using habitat, resource use, activity, and shelter;
6. mark unsupported details as uncertain;
7. reveal the JCEC provisional field name only after the reconstruction is complete.

The field-name reveal is intentionally not the “answer.” Students can produce different defensible reconstructions if their features are consistent with the evidence.

## Teacher interpretation guide by continent
These are intended interpretations, not exact drawing requirements.

### Gyre — Ridgeback Grazer
Likely evidence-supported ideas:
- broad feet for loose/uneven alpine ground;
- plant-feeding/grinding mouth structures inferred from cropped/crushed vegetation;
- dense insulating covering;
- shelter-seeking behaviour during high winds;
- movement between water and low vegetation;
- provisional niche: large alpine herbivore/grazer concentrated in productive valleys.

### Brillig — Canopy Glider
Likely evidence-supported ideas:
- flexible gliding membrane between limbs;
- gripping digits for wet branches;
- fruit feeding / seed handling;
- movement primarily within dense canopy;
- cavity shelter during heavy rain;
- provisional niche: arboreal canopy consumer using fruit resources and tree cavities.

### Manxome — Tide Skipper
Likely evidence-supported ideas:
- broad/gripping feet suited to wet rock;
- water-resistant body surface;
- scraping feeding structure for resources on rocks;
- movement between shallow water and sheltered cracks;
- storm avoidance / movement to protected coves;
- provisional niche: coastal shoreline grazer/consumer dependent on tidal water and rock shelter.

### Slithy Toves — Dune Runner
Likely evidence-supported ideas:
- broad feet for loose sand;
- dawn/evening or nighttime activity to avoid midday heat;
- burrow use for thermal refuge;
- plant/seed feeding;
- limited visits to surface water, suggesting water-conserving behaviour or other water sources;
- sheltered burrow placement during wind;
- provisional niche: desert ground consumer active during cooler periods near but not constantly at freshwater.

### Wabe — Snow Burrower
Likely evidence-supported ideas:
- compact body and small exposed ears reducing heat loss;
- thick pale insulation;
- broad front limbs for snow movement;
- sub-snow tunnel use as thermal shelter;
- food caching;
- reduced surface activity during strong wind;
- provisional niche: small subnivean consumer using snow cover as habitat and shelter.

### Bandersnatch — Plains Strider
Likely evidence-supported ideas:
- long limbs and long stride for sustained travel across prairie;
- elevated body/head useful in tall grass;
- grass/shoot feeding;
- group alert behaviour;
- seasonal movement toward water;
- provisional niche: mobile prairie grazer moving in groups across large seasonal ranges.

### Gimble — Barkclimber
Likely evidence-supported ideas:
- curved gripping digits and stiff tail brace for vertical climbing;
- feeding on small organisms beneath bark;
- tapping/probing behaviour before feeding;
- tree cavities as shelter;
- dependence on mature trees/fallen wood/complex forest structure;
- provisional niche: arboreal bark-foraging consumer associated with older forest habitat.

### Mimsy — Reedcrawler
Likely evidence-supported ideas:
- long spread toes distributing weight on mud/floating plants;
- water-resistant outer covering;
- feeding on small organisms at/near the water surface;
- ability to climb wetland vegetation;
- dense reeds used as shelter;
- greatest activity near the shallow-water/vegetation edge;
- provisional niche: small wetland consumer occupying the boundary between shallow water and emergent vegetation.

## Common misconceptions to watch for
### “The organism needed it, so it grew it.”
Redirect to: **How does this feature help? What evidence shows the fit between structure and environment?** Do not let the model imply intentional acquisition of adaptations.

### “Niche just means habitat.”
Habitat is where an organism lives. The provisional niche also includes resource use, activity patterns, shelter, and interactions.

### “Our drawing proves what it looks like.”
The drawing is a scientific reconstruction. Students should distinguish confirmed evidence, reasonable inference, and uncertainty.

### “Every adaptation must be perfect.”
The structure lab should show trade-offs. Useful structures can have limitations.

### “The JCEC field name means our drawing is wrong.”
The field name is only a working label. Multiple body reconstructions can remain plausible until stronger evidence becomes available.

## Continental Archive submission
One student submits for the team through the JCEC Submission Portal.

Use:
- **Submission Type:** Continental Archive
- **Mission Phase:** 01 First Contact
- **Operation / Task:** First Contact — Native Organism Profile
- **Continent:** assigned continent
- **Team ID:** teacher-assigned phase + continent + team number, e.g. `P1-GY-01`
- **Team Members:** everyone represented by the submission

The archive entry should include:
1. JCEC provisional field name;
2. concise niche summary;
3. most important structural/behavioural adaptations;
4. at least three pieces of First Contact evidence;
5. reasoning linking adaptation to environmental conditions and organism needs;
6. one uncertainty or next question.

## Individual accountability
After the team archive is submitted, every student answers:
- Which clue gave the strongest evidence for an adaptation?
- Which part of the reconstruction is least certain?
- How did the hands-on structure lab affect their reasoning?
- What evidence is still needed to understand the organism’s full ecosystem role?

Keep these in the Pathfinder Log by default. Collect selected reflections through JCEC when individual assessment evidence is needed.

## Assessment opportunities
Formative evidence can include:
- fair testing and accurate data recording in the lab;
- interpretation of structure-function trade-offs;
- distinction between observation and inference;
- evidence-supported adaptation claims;
- quality of niche description;
- willingness to identify uncertainty;
- scientific labels and reasoning on the field sketch;
- strength of archive evidence/reasoning.

Suggested recurring rubric dimensions:
- Science Understanding
- Evidence
- Reasoning
- Communication / Decision-Making

## Next narrative step
Operation 03 answers: **What kind of organism lives here, and how does it fit this environment?**

Operation 04 will ask: **What does this organism depend on, what depends on it, and how does energy move through the larger ecosystem?**

Do not reveal later invasive-species, contamination, succession, or settlement-decision twists during Operation 03.
