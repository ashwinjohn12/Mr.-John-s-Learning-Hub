# Unit 4 Build Standard — Grade 6 Math

Status: **LOCKED**

Unit: **04 · Operations on Decimals & Fractions**
Primary curriculum/content reference: **Dynamic Math G6 Teacher Resource, Chapter 4**
Prototype lesson: **4.1 Adding & Subtracting Fractions**

This document is the build contract for Lessons 4.2–4.6. Lesson 4.1 is the approved prototype. Future Unit 4 lessons should preserve its student-facing structure, pacing philosophy, assessment separation, and interaction quality while adapting the mathematics and Explore experience to each topic.

---

## A. Locked Unit 4 Lesson Standard

Every Unit 4 lesson must use this sequence:

1. Overview
2. Big Ideas
3. Understand
4. Examples
5. Vocabulary
6. Explore
7. Practise
8. Check Yourself
9. Apply
10. Review

### Student experience requirements

- Start with a short prediction or misconception hook. Students are not expected to know the answer yet.
- Overview must include prerequisite knowledge, student-friendly learning goals, time/materials, and success criteria.
- Include a clear student path through long lessons; lessons may be split into two sessions when appropriate.
- Big Ideas must state the conceptual reason behind the procedure before introducing an algorithm.
- Understand must use numbered concept blocks, concise language, visual/model support, and common mix-ups.
- Include a quick decision guide or strategy snapshot when students need to choose among methods.
- Examples must progress from core to more complex; advanced examples should be collapsible when the lesson is long or cognitively dense.
- Vocabulary should emphasize terms students need to reason and communicate, not every possible technical word.
- Explore must be an actual investigation/tool/game, not another multiple-choice quiz. It should teach by making students take meaningful mathematical actions.
- Practise must provide levels, hints, and explanatory feedback.
- Check Yourself must use a separate randomized bank and no Practice hints.
- Apply must be a realistic problem/challenge that requires choosing and explaining a strategy.
- Review must include summary, support, extension, PAT-style reasoning, checklist, and next-step handoff.
- Never expose project/build/developer language to students.
- Use current Alberta terminology, including **natural number** where curriculum alignment matters.
- Do not teach a shortcut or mnemonic before the underlying place-value/fraction meaning is established.

### Assessment standard

For each Unit 4 lesson:

- Supported Practice: approximately 8 high-quality questions spanning Foundations / Standard / Challenge.
- Check Yourself: target 18-question independent bank, randomized 5 at a time.
- Banks must include procedural fluency, representation/model interpretation, error analysis, estimation/reasonableness, and applications where appropriate.
- Practice and Check Yourself must not duplicate the same question set.
- PAT-style item should reward explanation/reasoning rather than only computation.

### Unit Review standard (to build after 4.6)

- Balanced coverage across all six lessons.
- Review vocabulary drawn from the whole unit, not only fractions or only decimals.
- Mixed supported practice.
- Stratified randomized Unit Check so each lesson is represented.
- Math Arcade must use real-world/game scenarios and be clearly different from Practice and Check Yourself.
- Include estimation/reasonableness and multi-step expression reasoning across the unit.

# B. Lesson 4.2 — Multiplying Fractions

**Curriculum anchor:** 6N7, with 6A1.1 connection.

## Critical curriculum guardrail

Despite the lesson title, Grade 6 does **not** teach fraction × fraction here. The focus is:

- natural number × fraction
- fraction × natural number
- natural number × unit fraction
- fraction of a natural-number quantity
- multiplication by a unit fraction as division
- problem solving and order of operations involving these ideas

Fraction × fraction belongs to later learning and must not be introduced as a Grade 6 algorithm.

## Big Idea

Multiplying a natural number and a fraction can mean either **repeated groups of a fraction** or **taking a fractional part of a quantity**. Multiplication does not always make a number larger.

## Prerequisites

- identify and represent unit fractions
- equivalent fractions and simplest form
- proper/improper fractions and mixed numbers
- multiplication/division facts
- understand product and factor
- add fractions with common/related denominators from 4.1

## Concept progression

1. Natural number × unit fraction as repeated addition.
2. Predict whether the product is proper, improper, or a natural number by comparing the natural number to the denominator.
3. Natural number × any fraction as repeated groups.
4. Fraction × natural number as “a fraction of a quantity.”
5. Compare the two interpretations: “groups of” versus “part of.”
6. Explain why multiplication by a proper fraction makes a positive natural number smaller, while an improper fraction can make it larger.
7. Connect multiplication by a unit fraction to division by its denominator.
8. Apply order of operations in short fraction contexts.

## Key misconceptions

- multiplying both numerator and denominator by the natural number
- believing multiplication always makes a number larger
- treating “4 groups of 2/5” and “2/5 of 4” as identical models even though the numerical product is equal
- assuming the denominator changes during repeated addition
- failing to simplify an improper result
- drifting into fraction × fraction procedures

## Worked-example progression

1. Repeated addition → multiplication statement: 5 groups of a unit fraction.
2. Natural × unit fraction with prediction of answer type.
3. Natural × non-unit fraction with model.
4. Fraction of a natural quantity using money or a collection.
5. Unit fraction multiplication ↔ division.
6. Multi-step application using parentheses/order of operations.

## Vocabulary

Unit fraction; factor; product; numerator; denominator; simplest form; repeated addition; commutative property; quotient; “fraction of a quantity.”

## Explore prototype

**Fraction Multiplier Lab**

Part A — **Groups Builder**: students build repeated groups of a fraction on a number line or strip and translate the model into multiplication.

Part B — **Part-of Machine**: students choose a fraction of a natural-number collection/money amount and visually select the required fraction.

Part C — **Product Predictor**: before calculating, students predict whether the answer will be less than, equal to, or greater than the natural number and justify using the size of the fractional factor.

Part D — **Divide Connection**: pair expressions such as 12 × 1/4 and 12 ÷ 4, then explain why they match.

## Practice/Check emphasis

- translate repeated addition ↔ multiplication
- natural × unit fraction
- natural × non-unit fraction
- fraction of a quantity
- product-size reasoning
- simplest form
- unit-fraction/division connection
- one or two order-of-operations/application items

## Apply challenge

A **recipe/event planner** where students scale fractional amounts for several batches and determine a fraction of a total supply, then explain which interpretation of multiplication they used.

# C. Lesson 4.3 — Relating Fractions to Equal Shares

**Curriculum anchor:** 6N5, with order-of-operations connection.

## Big Idea

A fraction can represent a **quotient**: the numerator is the quantity being shared and the denominator is the number of equal shares. The same quotient can often be represented as a fraction, mixed number, or decimal, and the best form depends on context.

## Prerequisites

- unit fractions
- improper fractions and mixed numbers
- simplest form and equivalent fractions
- multiplication of a fraction and natural number from 4.2
- product, factor, quotient
- basic fraction-decimal familiarity

## Concept progression

1. Model one whole shared equally among several people.
2. Model several wholes shared among fewer/more people using multiple representations.
3. Write equal sharing as a fraction: quantity ÷ number of shares = numerator/denominator.
4. Write a fraction as a division statement and vice versa.
5. Interpret improper fractions/mixed numbers as equal shares greater than one.
6. Decide when an exact fraction is more useful than a decimal approximation.
7. Convert a fraction quotient to decimal form using division.
8. Explain the vinculum/fraction bar as a division symbol.
9. Represent multi-step equal-sharing contexts with one complete numerical expression.

## Key misconceptions

- reversing numerator and denominator in a sharing situation
- thinking equal sharing must produce a natural-number answer
- believing a remainder is always simply “left over” rather than context-dependent
- assuming a decimal is always more accurate than a fraction
- treating a repeating decimal approximation as exactly equal to the original fraction without qualification
- writing disconnected calculations rather than an expression that represents the whole situation

## Worked-example progression

1. One chocolate bar shared among four people using model + fraction.
2. Five pizzas shared among four families using three methods.
3. Five bars shared among nine athletes; discuss exact fraction versus repeating decimal.
4. Fraction bar/vinculum → division statement.
5. Convert a quotient/fraction to a terminating decimal using division.
6. Equal-sharing word problem represented by one expression with parentheses.

## Vocabulary

Equal sharing; quotient; numerator; denominator; fraction; improper fraction; mixed number; decimal; vinculum; remainder; exact; approximation.

## Explore prototype

**Fair Share Studio**

Part A — **Share the Wholes**: distribute objects among people before partitioning leftovers.

Part B — **Cut First**: partition every whole into equal pieces first and compare with Part A.

Part C — **Fraction ↔ Division Translator**: convert scenarios, fractions, and division statements among forms.

Part D — **Best Representation**: choose fraction, mixed number, or decimal for a context and explain why.

## Practice/Check emphasis

- model equal shares
- write scenario → fraction
- fraction ↔ division statement
- improper fraction/mixed-number sharing
- fraction → decimal using division
- exact vs approximate representation
- order-of-operations sharing expressions
- interpretation/error-analysis questions

## Apply challenge

A **community food-share station** where quantities of food/drink must be shared equally among changing group sizes; students must show at least two representations and choose the most useful final form.

# D. Lesson 4.4 — Adding & Subtracting Decimal Numbers

**Curriculum anchor:** 6N2.

## Big Idea

Addition and subtraction work the same for very large numbers and very small decimal numbers because the standard algorithms are built on **place value**. Digits must be combined with digits representing the same units.

## Prerequisites

- decimal place value
- standard addition/subtraction with natural numbers
- regrouping
- reading money and metric measurements
- estimation/reasonableness from prior grades

## Concept progression

1. Connect natural-number calculations to decimal versions scaled by powers of ten.
2. Use place value to explain why decimal points align vertically in standard algorithms.
3. Add decimal numbers with different numbers of decimal places.
4. Use placeholder zeros to make place values visible when helpful.
5. Subtract decimal numbers with regrouping across the decimal point.
6. Use the same algorithm with very large natural numbers and precise decimals.
7. Solve money, metric measurement, population, time, and change problems.
8. Check reasonableness with estimation and place-value relationships.

## Key misconceptions

- lining numbers up by the last digit instead of place value/decimal point
- treating trailing zeros as changing the value
- dropping or moving the decimal point after computation
- regrouping without understanding the place-value exchange
- assuming a larger-looking string of digits is necessarily a larger decimal value
- choosing an operation only from a keyword instead of the situation

## Worked-example progression

1. Natural-number addition/subtraction and its decimal-scaled analogue.
2. Decimal addition with unequal decimal lengths.
3. Decimal subtraction requiring placeholder zeros/regrouping.
4. Money/change problem with multiple items.
5. Metric/race-time comparison requiring interpretation of “improvement.”

## Vocabulary

Place value; decimal point; sum; difference; addend; minuend; subtrahend; regroup; standard algorithm; estimate; reasonable.

## Explore prototype

**Decimal Alignment Lab**

Part A — **Place-Value Dock**: drag digits into a place-value chart and align decimal points before calculating.

Part B — **Zero Placeholder Test**: compare values such as 12.45, 12.450, and 12.4500 and decide when zeros help an algorithm without changing value.

Part C — **Decimal Error Detective**: diagnose misaligned addition/subtraction work.

Part D — **Scale Connection**: compare 153 + 481 with 15.3 + 48.1 and explain the factor-of-ten relationship.

## Practice/Check emphasis

- place-value alignment
- addition/subtraction fluency
- regrouping
- missing decimal point/error analysis
- money/change
- metric and time contexts
- comparing scaled calculations
- reasonableness

## Apply challenge

A **school event budget / timing board** combining purchases, change, measurement, and race-time data; students must identify the needed operation and justify place-value alignment.

# E. Lesson 4.5 — Multiplying Decimal Numbers

**Curriculum anchor:** 6N4, with 6A1.1 connection.

## Big Idea

Decimal multiplication is natural-number multiplication applied to **place-value units** such as tenths and hundredths. Estimation should predict the size of the product before or after using a standard algorithm.

## Scope guardrail

The curriculum focus is multiplying up to 3-digit natural or decimal numbers by **2-digit natural numbers**. Do not turn this lesson into a general decimal × decimal algorithm lesson beyond what the source models to support place-value understanding.

## Prerequisites

- multiplication facts
- multi-digit natural-number multiplication
- area models / expanded form
- decimal place value
- repeated addition
- estimation/rounding
- money notation

## Concept progression

1. Repeated addition of a decimal amount → multiplication.
2. Estimate products using friendly/rounded numbers and upper/lower bounds.
3. Interpret decimal factors as tenths/hundredths: e.g., 2.1 = 21 tenths.
4. Use an area model to multiply a decimal by a natural number.
5. Use expanded form / partial products.
6. Connect expanded form to the standard algorithm.
7. Explain where the decimal placement comes from through place-value units, not a “count decimal places” trick.
8. Compare multiple valid strategies.
9. Solve money and multi-step order-of-operations problems.

## Key misconceptions

- blindly moving/placing the decimal point without place-value reasoning
- forgetting the scale difference between 10.3 and 103
- believing a calculator-like procedure matters more than estimating the product size
- adding contextual quantities before interpreting the operations
- treating carried digits as meaningless marks rather than place-value amounts

## Worked-example progression

1. Estimate a product such as 6 × 4.71.
2. Money example such as 5 × $1.25.
3. Decimal × 2-digit natural number with area model.
4. Same product with standard algorithm/place-value explanation.
5. Multi-step wage/tips or measurement context.

## Vocabulary

Product; factor; partial product; area model; expanded form; standard algorithm; estimate; front-end estimation; reasonable; tenths; hundredths.

## Explore prototype

**Decimal Product Lab**

Part A — **Estimate First**: choose a reasonable interval before exact calculation.

Part B — **Place-Value Builder**: reinterpret decimal numbers as tenths/hundredths and multiply those units.

Part C — **Area Model Machine**: decompose both factors and combine partial products.

Part D — **Algorithm Translator**: match each row of a standard algorithm to the partial product/place-value meaning.

## Practice/Check emphasis

- estimation intervals
- decimal × one/two-digit natural numbers
- area model ↔ algorithm
- place-value scaling
- money
- multi-step expressions
- error analysis of decimal placement
- reasonableness

## Apply challenge

A **school fundraiser / job earnings simulator** with repeated costs or earnings, quantities, and a final multi-step total. Students estimate before calculating and explain why the exact product is reasonable.

# F. Lesson 4.6 — Dividing Decimal Numbers

**Curriculum anchor:** 6N4, with 6A1.1 connection.

## Big Idea

Decimal division is based on equal sharing, multiplication facts, and place value. Standard algorithms are efficient representations of those ideas. A remainder must be interpreted according to the situation and can sometimes be expressed as a decimal.

## Scope guardrail

Focus on dividing up to 3-digit natural or decimal numbers by **2-digit natural numbers**. Do not introduce division by a decimal divisor as the main Grade 6 skill.

## Prerequisites

- division/multiplication facts
- natural-number division algorithms
- decimal place value
- area model/partial quotients
- equal sharing from 4.3
- estimation with compatible numbers
- meaning of quotient/remainder

## Concept progression

1. Decimal division as equal sharing using money/base-10 blocks.
2. Connect natural-number and decimal quotients by powers of ten: 96 ÷ 4 versus 9.6 ÷ 4.
3. Use area models/partial quotients to decompose dividends.
4. Interpret decimals as tenths/hundredths during division.
5. Connect models to the standard/long-division algorithm.
6. Divide by two-digit natural divisors.
7. Express a remainder as a decimal when the context allows continuous sharing.
8. Interpret remainders differently by context: leftovers, round up, or continue into decimals.
9. Estimate quotients with compatible numbers and use the estimate as a reasonableness check.
10. Solve money, rate, measurement, and sharing problems.

## Key misconceptions

- assuming every remainder should be written the same way
- always rounding a quotient up or down regardless of context
- losing place value when zeros are appended to continue division
- moving decimal points by rule without explaining the units
- choosing incompatible numbers that make an estimate harder
- forgetting that a decimal dividend can be interpreted as a number of tenths/hundredths

## Worked-example progression

1. 0.8 ÷ 4 using base-10/equal sharing.
2. 9.6 ÷ 4 using area model and place-value units.
3. 15.6 ÷ 12 using area model + standard algorithm.
4. Natural-number quotient with remainder converted to decimal form.
5. Three-context remainder comparison (left over / round up / divide further).
6. Estimate a quotient using compatible numbers.
7. Money or distance-per-hour problem.

## Vocabulary

Dividend; divisor; quotient; remainder; compatible numbers; estimate; partial quotient; standard algorithm; decimal; tenths; hundredths; reasonable.

## Explore prototype

**Decimal Division Lab**

Part A — **Equal Share Board**: distribute base-10 value among groups.

Part B — **Place-Value Shift Connection**: compare paired problems such as 925 ÷ 25 and 92.5 ÷ 25.

Part C — **Remainder Decision Room**: students choose whether a real-world remainder should stay left over, force another group/unit, or continue as a decimal.

Part D — **Compatible Number Estimator**: choose a useful nearby dividend/divisor pair before exact division.

Part E — **Algorithm Stepper**: reveal one long-division step at a time and connect each step to multiplication/subtraction/place value.

## Practice/Check emphasis

- natural/decimal division by natural divisors
- area model/partial quotient
- standard algorithm
- remainders as decimals
- context-sensitive remainder interpretation
- compatible-number estimation
- place-value scaling
- money/rate/measurement problems
- reasonableness/error analysis

## Apply challenge

A **trip/event logistics challenge**: split costs, food, travel distances, or supplies among groups; students must decide how to interpret each remainder and justify when a decimal, whole-number rounding, or leftover is appropriate.

# G. Whole-Unit Progression

The six lessons should feel like one journey:

- **4.1 — Same-sized fraction units:** rename before adding/subtracting.
- **4.2 — Fraction multiplication meaning:** groups of / part of / unit fraction as division.
- **4.3 — Fraction as quotient:** equal sharing connects fraction, division, mixed numbers, and decimals.
- **4.4 — Decimal addition/subtraction:** place-value algorithms transfer across number sizes.
- **4.5 — Decimal multiplication:** place-value units connect models, estimation, partial products, and the standard algorithm.
- **4.6 — Decimal division:** equal sharing + place value + estimation + context-sensitive remainders.

The conceptual bridge from 4.2 → 4.3 is essential. Lesson 4.2 introduces multiplication by a unit fraction as division; Lesson 4.3 develops fractions as quotients and equal shares. Lesson 4.3 then naturally prepares students to interpret decimal quotients before 4.4–4.6 focus on decimal algorithms.

# H. Build Order

Build and QA lessons in this order:

1. 4.2 Multiplying Fractions
2. 4.3 Relating Fractions to Equal Shares
3. 4.4 Adding & Subtracting Decimal Numbers
4. 4.5 Multiplying Decimal Numbers
5. 4.6 Dividing Decimal Numbers
6. Whole-Unit consistency audit
7. Unit 4 Review & Math Arcade

Do not batch-generate all remaining lessons before checking 4.2 and 4.3. They establish the fraction-to-division conceptual bridge that the decimal portion of the unit depends on.
