# Jabberwocky Phase 1 — Operation 07 Teacher Guide

## Classified Incident: The Stowaway

### Purpose
Operation 07 is the major ecological twist in Phase 1. Students have already learned how normal population change, disturbance, succession, and recovery can be monitored. JCEC now discovers two human-caused problems that do **not** fit ordinary recovery:

1. an Earth-origin organism has escaped containment; and
2. a persistent fictional maintenance contaminant, X-17, is moving through the food web.

The operation brings together introduced species, invasive-species evidence, human impacts, pollution, bioaccumulation/biomagnification, monitoring, unintended consequences, and evidence-based environmental management.

Central message:

**Human impacts can be unintended and still require evidence-based responsibility.**

Students should not learn “all introduced organisms are invasive” or “every pollutant biomagnifies.” The page explicitly requires evidence of spread + harm before using the invasive label, and X-17 is clearly identified as a fictional persistent contaminant used to model biomagnification.

## Alberta Grade 7 Science alignment
Operation 07 supports Interactions and Ecosystems by asking students to:
- examine human-caused ecosystem change;
- distinguish intended from unintended consequences;
- investigate introduced/exotic organisms and possible ecological impacts;
- interpret monitoring evidence showing spread and changes in native organisms/resources;
- relate competition and habitat change to an introduced organism;
- interpret a pollutant moving through a food web;
- recognize that persistent contaminants can accumulate in organisms and become more concentrated at higher feeding levels;
- use multiple evidence sources before making an environmental-management decision;
- consider benefits, risks, uncertainty, and unintended consequences of management actions;
- propose prevention, containment, restoration, and monitoring strategies.

## Hands-on Investigation A: Stowaway Spread Simulation
### Materials per team
- six cups or paper zones labelled Cargo, A, B, C, D, E;
- ~30 neutral counters;
- four visibly different “stowaway” counters;
- one spoon/scoop;
- Operation 07 Incident Record.

### Round A — no checkpoint
Students randomly transfer counters Cargo → A → B → C → D → E, recording which zones receive a stowaway.

### Round B — checkpoint
Reset. Students inspect each transfer and remove visible stowaway counters to quarantine before the counters enter the next zone.

### Debrief
Ask:
- Why did the checkpoint work best before widespread movement?
- What does the model leave out?
- How could real organisms spread even if a visible inspection works imperfectly?
- Why might seeds, eggs, spores, microbes, or soil be harder to detect than these counters?

Do not imply that inspection can reduce real biosecurity risk to zero. The model isolates the concept that prevention/early interception can interrupt human-created pathways.

## Science concepts
### Introduced vs invasive
An introduced species has been moved by humans outside its previous range. Students need evidence of **spread and ecological harm** before defending the invasive-threat label.

A team may reasonably select “uncertain” when the evidence shows concern but does not fully isolate causation. The grading target is the evidence chain, not a required word.

### Bioaccumulation vs biomagnification
- **Bioaccumulation:** buildup inside one organism over time.
- **Biomagnification:** increasing concentration across feeding levels.

The token activity demonstrates biomagnification under a simplified assumption: the contaminant persists and is transferred with food.

### X-17
X-17 is fictional. Do not present its numerical values as real toxicological thresholds. Students are interpreting the **pattern**, not deciding whether a real chemical concentration is medically or environmentally safe.

## Hands-on Investigation B: Biomagnification Token Model
### Materials
- 16 producer cards;
- 4 primary-consumer cards;
- 1 predator card;
- 16 contaminant tokens.

### Sequence
1. Give each producer one token.
2. Each primary consumer “eats” four producers and receives those four tokens.
3. The predator “eats” the four consumers and receives all 16 tokens.
4. Compare contaminant amount per organism at each level.

### Debrief
Students should be able to explain:
- why the predator ends with more contaminant per individual;
- why low environmental concentration does not automatically mean low concentration in top consumers;
- why the result depends on persistence and feeding transfer;
- why biomagnification is not a rule for every substance.

## Team workflow
Recommended roles:
- **Spread Tracker** — records occupied zones and trends;
- **Native-Impact Checker** — identifies evidence of ecological harm;
- **Food-Web Tracer** — follows X-17 through feeding levels;
- **Response Challenger** — asks about alternative explanations and unintended effects.

Teams should follow the standing collaboration standard:

**Everyone explores → Team discusses → One official team decision → Each student reflects.**

## Continental incident interpretations
These are intended interpretations, not scripts.

### Gyre — Earth Ryegrass
Evidence shows spread from human movement/runoff corridors plus declining Bluecrest cover in overlap zones. Strong responses combine route restriction/cleaning, targeted removal, and continued monitoring. Avoid assuming all Ridgeback effects are already known.

### Brillig — Earth Compost Worm
The strongest evidence is faster litter loss and reduced seedling/litter habitat in occupied plots. Large-scale soil disturbance could create additional harm, so targeted containment/experiments are defensible.

### Manxome — Earth Shore Snail
Spread into sheltered algae habitat coincides with added grazing pressure. The earlier high-wave decline cannot explain the current sheltered-cove pattern. Island-to-island transport is an important future risk.

### Slithy Toves — Earth Drought Grass
This case emphasizes that the introduced species is benefiting from a **human-created water pathway**. A strong explanation may identify both the plant and water-management practice as causes. Broad disturbance of desert soil can be worse than targeted control.

### Wabe — Earth Coldgrass
The introduced grass is favoured by disturbed warmer/wetter ruts rather than intact tundra. Students may reasonably prioritize habitat repair, targeted removal, or a controlled comparison. Avoid heavy restoration machinery as an automatic solution.

### Bandersnatch — Earth Brome Grass
This is the clearest fast-spread prairie case. Native cover and richness decline in dense invasion patches; animal/human movement can move seeds. Students should consider invasion-front control and native-prairie protection.

### Gimble — Earth Bark Beetle
DNA/packing-wood evidence strongly supports the introduction pathway. Native bark habitat and food-web effects are beginning to appear. Aggressive tree removal can itself damage forest habitat, so mapping/targeted containment matters.

### Mimsy — Earth Marsh Grass
The introduced grass is spreading along a water-flow path changed by humans. Habitat restoration and species containment are linked; treating only the plant without considering hydrology may fail.

## Incident-response choices
Each continent has three plausible response strategies. None is auto-graded.

Look for teams to discuss:
- preventing further spread/source release;
- containment versus broad removal;
- risk of damaging native habitat during intervention;
- restoring the human-altered condition that favours the introduced species;
- monitoring invasion fronts and native indicators;
- uncertainty and thresholds for stronger action.

- **Continent:** assigned continent
- **Team ID:** teacher assigned, e.g. `P1-GY-01`

## Individual reflection
Students explain:
- strongest decision-changing evidence;
- introduced vs invasive;
- biomagnification using their own food-web pathway;
- one prevention/biosecurity/pollution rule humans should follow.

## Common misconceptions
### “Introduced = invasive.”
Require spread + harm evidence.

### “The introduced organism is bad because it is from Earth.”
Origin alone is not ecological harm.

### “If two trends change together, the stowaway caused everything.”
Ask for mechanism, comparison evidence, timing, and alternatives.

### “Biomagnification means the environment has more pollutant than the predator.”
The comparison is concentration in organisms across feeding levels.

### “Every pollutant biomagnifies.”
No. The model assumes persistence and feeding transfer.

### “Removing everything immediately is always best.”
Intervention can disturb habitat. Require trade-off reasoning.
